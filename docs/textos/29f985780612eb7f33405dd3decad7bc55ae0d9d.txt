Perceived changes in transformational leadership: the role of motivation and perceived
skills in educational leadership training under an EU-China cooperation project



Abstract

This study aims to examine the relationship among motivation for joining an educational leadership training

programme (MOT), perceived skills (PS), and perceived changes in transformational leadership (PCTFL) in

educational leadership training. The study drew on survey data from 761 participants who were associated with a

leadership development program under an EU-China cooperation project. The conceptual model was tested by

structural equation modelling (SEM) by the Mplus programme. MOT and PCTFL were measured respectively by

second order factor structure assessments due to their multi-dimensional nature. The findings of the study suggest

that motivation positively influences perceived changes in transformational leadership, and this relationship is

positively mediated by perceived skills as learning gains in the educational leadership training. The study provides

novel empirical evidence on motivation that leads to transformational leadership changes in leadership training

programmes. Policymakers and other key stakeholders can use these findings to gain a deeper understanding of

the success of projects and encourage academic members to participate in leadership training programmes to

improve transformational leadership.


Keywords: Perceived skills, Motivation, Leadership style, Leadership training, Transformational leadership



1. Introduction



Researchers and practitioners alike have recognized the pivotal importance of effective leadership in guiding,

motivating, and cultivating high-performing organizations (Jacobsen et al., 2022). Given the rapidly evolving

landscape of higher education (HE), new leadership styles that foster innovation, positive change, and growth are

increasingly necessary (Khorshid et al., 2023). As a result, there has been a growing interest in studying positive

leadership styles and their potential impact on followers’ attitudes and behaviours in HE (Aboramadan et al.,

2020). It has been identified that the transformational leadership serves as a prerequisite for the HE sector to

navigate the ever-changing internal and external environment (Khorshid et al., 2023). As a positive leadership

style, transformational leadership has a favourable impact on organisational climate, subordinates, students,

faculty members (Khorshid et al., 2023), and enhancing the capacity of universities to achieve their goals

(Khorshid et al., 2023). Nevertheless, studies have highlighted the lack of systematic development of leadership

skills and styles in academia (Abdulla et al.,2022). As a consequence, leaders in academic settings are often

unprepared for their roles (Abdulla et al., 2022), making leadership development programmes a critical aspect of

improving leadership skills and leadership styles (Zhu & Zayim-Kurtay, 2018; Cheng & Zhu, 2021, 2023; Cheng

et al., 2023). While previous studies have found that leadership training can improve overall leader effectiveness

or performance (Jacobsen et al., 2022; Day & Sin, 2011; Kragt & Guenter, 2018). Still, the understanding of how

leadership training can result in specific effectiveness in terms of leadership styles remains rudimentary. Hence,

this study aims to examine how participants’ motivation for joining the leadership training programme (MOT)

and their perceived skills (PS) affect their perceived changes in transformational leadership (PCTFL) after the

training.




The comprehensive view of motivation offered by self-determination theory (SDT) (Ryan & Deci, 2017) has the

capacity to provide insights into the underlying motivators of various leadership styles and contribute to the

advancement of leadership research. Recognizing the drivers of motivation can be of great value to practitioners

and researchers interested in exploring leadership readiness (Boyce et al., 2010; Kanat-Maymon et al., 2020).

Prior research indicates that training and feedback are effective strategies for modifying leadership behaviours,

suggesting that an amalgamation of coaching, classroom instruction, feedback, and experiential training can have

a substantial influence on leaders’ performance (Seidle et al., 2016). Moreover, a persistent rise in self-reported

transformational leadership following an identical exercise leader development was found by Eid et al. (2008) and

Johnsen et al. (2009). To understand the factors that influence leadership styles during this process, certain

researchers have emphasized the role of motivation (Chan & Drasgow, 2001; Kanat-Maymon et al., 2020). Yet,

we know little about whether the motivation of participants for joining leadership training naturally impacts on

behaviours in a transformational manner after leadership training.




Additionally, we propose perceived skills (as a learning gain in the training programme) to mediate the

relationship between motivation and the PCTFL. This is because uplifting knowledge and skills are the motivation

of academics joining a leadership training programme (Kao et al. 2011; Loizzo et al. 2017). The very core of

specialized leadership training lies a focus on enhancing leaders’ knowledge, skills, and attitudes; finally, on

practices of leadership styles (Bass, 1999). Although scholars have acknowledged leaders who train on the

dimensions of leadership skills are much more successful in implementing them and integrating them into practice

(Afshari et al., 2012). There is currently no existing research on the mediating role of perceived skills. The

conceptual model for the present study is illustrated in Figure 1.




The present research makes three distinct contributions to existing literature. First, previous study did not explore

the reasons behind individuals’ participation in leadership trainings, which is crucial in order to eliminate

alternative explanations for the effectiveness of such leadership trainings (Kragt & Guenter, 2018). In this study,

we investigated participants’ motivation, perceived skills and the PCTFL. Second, we introduce and examine

perceived skills as a motivational mechanism that elucidates the connection between MOT and PCTFL. This

approach addresses the recent calls to explore additional mediators in research (Kaše et al., 2019). Third, we

propose and test the relationship between perceived skills and perceived changes in transformational leadership.

In this way, this study is an echo of recent appeals to investigate more immediate indicators of leadership training,

as well as other related outcomes (Jacobsen et al., 2022).




2. Theoretical background and hypotheses formation

2.1 Conceptualisation

2.1.1 Educational leadership and its training in HE context

Educational leadership at higher educational institutions (HEIs) is conceptualized as having a significant impact

on students, researchers and fellow educators through activities such as mentoring, disseminating scholarship,

sharing knowledge, fostering innovation, creating social support networks, and providing social services (Cheng

& Zhu, 2021). Leadership training programmes is defined as programmes that have been systematically designed

to improve the knowledge, skills, competencies, and other elements of a leader (Lacerenza et al., 2017). In the

leadership training programme as the context of this research, most of the participants are academics who take up

the roles as educational leaders (Cheng & Zhu, 2021), educational leadership was covered in the training content.

In this study, the research focuses on the educational leadership training aspects of the leadership development

program.




2.1.2 Transformational leadership style

Transformational leaders seek to improve followers’ motivation (Avolio & Bass, 2002) and ethical behaviour

(Bass, 1999), exhibit charisma, developing a vision, respect and trust (Sousa & Rocha, 2019). They exhibit

proactivity by raising followers’ awareness, assisting them in achieving extraordinary goals and challenge

followers with new ideas and approaches (Sousa & Rocha, 2019; Mustafa, 2020). As research have shown that

transformational leadership can lead to a variety of positive outcomes in educational settings (Mustafa, 2020), it

is crucial to undergo training that facilitates success and effectiveness in adopting transformational leadership

styles (Bass, 1999; Ytterstad et al., 2020), hence, it is important to focus on improving transformational leadership

in leadership training, and to develop the key skills and behaviours associated with this leadership style (Ytterstad

et al., 2020).




2.2 Theoretical framework



Kirkpatrick model for the leadership training and self-determination theory



In this study, Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model (Kirkpatrick & Kirkpatrick, 2006), encompassing four-level of

evaluation - reaction, learning, behavioural change, and results - serves as the framework for understanding

learners’ learning outcomes (Figure 1). The first two levels are considered short-term. Reaction refers to affective

satisfaction and utility perceptions of training contents, which affect trainees’ motivation to apply the trained

contents on the job. Learning refers to the extension of skills (i.e., knowledge, ability, and attitudes). During this

phase, participants acquire knowledge of leadership competencies through theoretical learning, while also

engaging in practical application by taking on various leadership roles during training. This experiential learning

allows for a deeper understanding of leadership competencies, resulting in perceived skills as a learning outcome

in the “learning” element of Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model (Kirkpatrick & Kirkpatrick, 2006). The last two levels

are understood as medium and long-term effects in the work environment: Behaviour corresponds to the

application of training contents, and results refer to positive effects on organizational success (e.g., costs, turnover,

absenteeism) through improved performance. In this study, we evaluate the training outcomes from learning to

behavioural change, namely, perceived changes in their transformational leadership style (Eze, 2011).


Drawing on SDT (Ryan & Deci, 2017), the motivation holds the potential to elucidate the outcomes of leadership

training programmes (Figure 1). Research also found that the motivation for joining the training is centrally

important for attaining behavioural changes after the training (Gegenfurtner & Vauras, 2012). Training should

focus on enhancing leader’s knowledge, skills, and leadership styles (Bass, 1999) and improve leaders’

transformational leadership skills (Afshari et al., 2012). Leaders who train on the dimensions of leadership skills

are much more successful in integrating them into learning and teaching (Afshari et al., 2012). In this study, as

the learning outcomes of leadership training programmes, the improvement of transformational leadership style

thus can be influenced by the antecedents of motivation for joining the leadership training.

2.3 Educational leadership training and behavioural changes


As per Eze’s (2011) definition, leadership style refers to the general pattern of behaviours exhibited by a leader.

Meta-analytical evidence suggests that leadership interventions have an overall positive effect on behavioural and

performance outcomes (d=0.48) (Avolio et al., 2009). Leadership training often include examples of leadership

behaviours (Kragt & Guenter, 2018), which also positively impacts leaders’ behavioural means (Day & Sin,

2011). In addition, meta-analytical evidence suggests that both affective training reactions and utility reactions

positively associate with the reported use of learned skills and behaviours (Kragt & Guenter, 2018). Despite this,

training providers are calling for research that will inform the design and execution phase of training initiative

that would eventually lead to behavioural changes (Nafukho et al., 2022), namely, to perceived changes in

leadership styles (Eze, 2011).

2.4 Motivation for joining leadership training in relation to the behavioural changes in transformational
leadership style




Motivation’s role in learning outcomes has been emphasized in the available literature (Ryan & Deci, 2017;

Douglas et al., 2020). Motivation for joining the training is centrally important for attaining behavioural changes

after the training (Gegenfurtner & Vauras, 2012). By behavioural changes or transfer of training, we mean the

productive application of the newly learned content of a training programme (Sasson & Miedijensky, 2023). The

motivation for joining the training is an important precursor to initiate learning activities (Beier & Kanfer, 2010),

therefore, affects whether or not trainees become engaged in the training (Brophy, 2010). Specifically,

participants’ motivation is positively linked to enthusiasm and engagement (Van et al., 2013), attitudes and

behaviours (Løvaas et al., 2020) in the training process, leaders who are motivated are likely to see activities as

their own reward and opportunities for learning and growth (Ryan & Deci, 2017). As adult learners, academics’

motivation for joining a leadership training course varies from uplifting their knowledge and skills to social

interaction (Kao et al. 2011; Loizzo et al. 2017). Understanding their motivation to participate in the learning and

how this relates to learning outcomes have been highlighted in the literature over the past decade (Douglas et al.

2020). The findings of Dinh et al. (2022) endorsed the view that the motivation has effects on learning outcomes

of the leadership training programme.


Such motivationally driven and metacognitively guided processing of the training and learning material is key for

application (Brophy, 2010) because the training content and skills are both connected to prior knowledge (Goulet-

Pelletier et al., 2023), which is a prerequisite for high-road application in educational contexts (Brophy, 2010).

Previous study (Dinh et al., 2022) explored the correlation between motivation for joining the training and the

perceived skills, regarding the perceived effectiveness of a leadership training programme in the context of the

EU and Chinese HE, which didn’t study further to the level of behavioral changes after the training. In response

to Dinh et al.’s (2022) call, this study attempted to measure motivation in a leadership training programme and its

relationship to the perceived skills and the behavioural changes in transformational leadership style after the

training. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are put forth.

Hypothesis 1. Motivation has a significant positive effect on perceived changes in transformational leadership

style.


Hypothesis 2. Motivation has a significant positive effect on perceived skills.




2.5 Perceived skills and behavioural changes in transformational leadership style


Competency and skills are generally defined as the capacity of an individual (or a collective) to successfully

resolve certain situations or achieve certain goals (Ellström & Kock, 2008). Research suggests that training should

focus on improving leaders’ transformational leadership skills (Afshari et al., 2012) and the very core of

specialized leadership training lies the emphasis on enhancing leader’s knowledge, skills, and leadership styles

(Bass, 1999), and leaders who train on the dimensions of leadership skills are much more successful in

implementing them and integrating them into learning and teaching (Afshari et al., 2012). When it comes to the

learning environment, skills are considered as a combination of cognitive, motivational, and social conditions that

are acquired for the success of training (Merino-Soto et al., 2022). Perceived skills, accordingly, is generally

conceptualised as perception of learners toward skills obtained in the training courses which, thereafter, are

transferred into practices (Kirkpatrick & Kirkpatrick, 2006). Several research highlights that exploring learners’

perception of skills is essential because it closely relates to behavioural actions (Dinh et al., 2022; Merino-Soto et

al., 2022). Based on the relationship between different competencies and behavioural changes have been found,

the following hypothesis is put forward.




Hypothesis 3. Perceived skills have a significant positive effect on perceived changes in transformational

leadership style.




2.6 The mediating role of perceived skills

To reiterate, based on SDT and Kirkpatrick’s evaluation model (Kirkpatrick & Kirkpatrick, 2006), we examine

the participants’ motivation for joining the leadership training, which later can impact the outcomes of the training

(Dinh et al., 2022). Leadership training participants are motivated to apply knowledge, abilities, and skills

acquired in their leadership practice (Bass, 2008). As learning outcomes, participants learn theoretically leadership

skills, they also put that into practice by playing different leadership roles during training, which allow them to

gain deeper learning of leadership skills, training, and education on leadership styles (Bass, 1999), leading to

PCTFL on the next level of “product” (Kirkpatrick & Kirkpatrick, 2006). In addition, a defining feature of

motivation is the congruence between behavioural changes and one’s own sense of agency, such that these

behaviours are perceived as originating from oneself (Deci & Ryan, 2000). Related research also found that the

motivation for joining the training is centrally important for attaining behavioural changes after the training

(Gegenfurtner & Vauras, 2012), predicating that the motivation for joining the leadership training programme can

impact on the PCTFL after training. As what the current study aimed at, previous studies have not uncovered the

triangular relationship between perceived skills, motivation to learn, and behavioural changes in educational

leadership training. The following hypothesis is put forth.




Hypothesis 4. Perceived skills positively mediate the relationship between motivation and perceived changes in

transformational leadership style.

3. Methods



3.1. Context of the study

This research is conducted as part of a capacity-building project in HE funded by the European Union’s Erasmus+

programme. Based on multilateral partnerships, Erasmus+ capacity-building projects in the field of HE are

transnational cooperative projects primarily between higher education institutions from EU or countries

associated to the Programme and third countries not associated to the Programme.


The EU-China capacity-building project in this study in HE is supported by the Erasmus+ programme, which

aims to: strengthen the capacity of higher education institutions in governance and academic leadership; enhance

the knowledge and capacities of academic leaders and potential academic leaders on university governance and

leadership, etc. Regarding EU-China partnership, this project has significant impact on further strengthening the

EU-China cooperation in university governance, supporting the modernisation and internationalisation of HEIs

and fostering innovative capacities of Chinese and European universities. It can also further contribute to the EU-

China High-Level People-to-People Dialogue (HPPD), especially the EU-China cooperation in higher education.


The leadership training programme in this study aimed at developing leadership skills for academics, with one of

the focuses on the development of educational leadership for both educational leaders and teaching staff members

in academic institutions. Through practice-based and community-based learning methods, the programmes were

developed with one of the main aims of enhancing the knowledge and skills of academic teaching staff and

aspiring educational leaders within academic institutions, as well as improving their transformational leadership

style. The present study presents quantitative findings from three online leadership training programmes

accompanied with live sessions from 2020 to 2022 and three blended leadership training programmes

implemented between 2021 and 2022.




3.2 Participants


There are altogether 785 participants who directly involved in the leadership training programmes between 2020

and 2022 filled in the survey. The participants attended different training sessions are not the same group of

participants, the sample size in this study is therefore the accumulated participants in all the trainings. The

participants encompassed researchers, lecturers, assistant professors, professors, academic directors, deans, etc.

After deleting the invalid responses, the demographic profile of the participants (N= 761) was analysed. Appendix

A shows the work-related leadership experience of academic members: ranged from 1 year to 5 years (n = 343;

45.1%), 6–10 years (n = 112; 14.7%), and over 10 years (n = 70; 9.2%), respectively. Among the respondents,

57.7% identified as male and 42.3% as female. In terms of age distribution, the majority of participants fell within

the 21-30 range, accounting for 44.7% of the sample, following by the 31-40 age group (34.4 %), and those above

41 years old (20.9 %).




3.3 Measures




3.3.1 Perceived changes in transformational leadership style


The perceived changes in transformational leadership style after the leadership training were measured through

the scale of transformational leadership styles in the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ) (Avolio &

Bass, 2004). The transformational leadership styles were adopted from previous research by Johnsen et al. (2009)

and validated by Løvaas et al. (2020). This scale includes 13-item multidimensional construct that comprised four

dimensions of PCTFL, including idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, and

individualized consideration.


3.3.2 Motivation for joining the leadership training


Motivation for joining the leadership training programme was adopted from previous research by Kao et al. (2011)

and validated by Dinh et al. (2022). This 11-item multidimensional construct comprised three dimensions,

including practical enhancement, occupational promotion, and social contact (Kao et al., 2011).


3.3.3 Perceived skills


Perceived skills in the leadership training programme were assessed using a four-item adapted scale which was

developed by Kirkpatrick and Kirkpatrick (2006) and validated by Dinh et al. (2022). Perceived skills refer to

leadership skills illustrated in the training for addressing the challenges which academics are facing in their

institution, enhancing leadership skills as an educational leader, such as showing empathy, communication skills,

etc., developing personal leadership styles, especially transformational leadership styles and building confidence

and engaging colleagues to develop and achieve academic visions in their institutions/ faculties.




In addition, five-point Likert scale (from 1= strongly disagree, to 5 = strongly agree) was used for measuring the

latent constructs (Zaman et al., 2022), namely MOT, PS and PCTFL. Second order factor structure assessments

(Zaman et al., 2022) were specifically employed to capture the multi-dimensional nature of motivation and

perceived changes in transformational leadership, respectively.




3.4 Data collection and procedure


Once the initial questionnaire (including scales of motivation for joining leadership training, scales of perceived

skills and scales of perceived changes in transformational leadership styles) was formulated, three experts were

enlisted to review and provide feedback on all the items. Subsequently, a pilot study was conducted with a small

group of participants to ascertain the validity and reliability of the modified instrument. The questionnaire was

sent to the participants by Qualtrics and Wenjuanxing tools after each training session from 2020-2022. The

analysis of reliability and validity from the pilot study informed the final survey, which comprised 36 questions

covering respondents’ background information and the main constructs of interest. Data was collected following

each training edition.


3.5 Data analysis


SPSS 28 was utilized for data screening and descriptive analysis. Followed by the utilization of Mplus 8.3, which

is a comprehensive programme for robust structural equation modelling (SEM) estimations, to perform

measurement validation and path model analysis. This was implemented by employing SEM (Muthén et al.,

2017). SEM is a potent multivariate statistical analysis that investigates structural connections among constructs

by scrutinizing and comprehending latent attributes and has been extensively endorsed by researchers (Dash &

Paul, 2021; Mueller & Hancock, 2019; Zaman, 2022). To that end, the two steps of model fit evaluation in SEM

were implemented: Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and structural model (Muthén et al., 2017). Frist, Mplus

was employed to assess composite reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity through CFA.

Furthermore, to verify the measurements, various model fit indices such as chi-square, comparative fit index

(CFI), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and

Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) (Mueller & Hancock, 2019; Dash & Paul, 2021). Subsequently, the structural model

was performed to test the hypotheses.




4. Findings




4.1 Measurement validation

In this study, we used a second-order factor structure for the constructs of MOT and PCTFL. Second-order model

of motivation demonstrated a target coefficient of 1, along with an overall reliability of 0.88. Similarly, second-

order model of perceived changes in transformational leadership exhibited a target coefficient of 0.96, with an

overall reliability of 0.95 indicating substantial evidence in support of a second-order construct (Marsh &

Hocevar, 1985). The Cronbach’s alpha, factor loading, composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted

(AVE) were used as indicators to assess the fitness of the measurement model. Appendix B illustrates the

constructs and sub-constructs with the corresponding number of measured items and their loadings. The items

having factor loadings greater than 0.6 (for CFA) were considered (Hair et al., 2016). Furthermore, the thresholds

recommended by Hair et al. (2016) were met, with CR > 0.70, AVE > 0.50, and Cronbach’s alpha > 0.70. Table

1 displays the results indicating all constructs met these thresholds without any concerns. Table 1 also provides

CR and AVEs results, revealing that there are no concerns regarding reliability and validity. Additionally,

multicollinearity and discriminant validity are also presented in Table 1, where square correlations in diagonals

are below the acceptable thresholds, indicating no validity concerns (Mueller & Hancock, 2019). Table 2 displays

that all fit indices for the SEM fall within the acceptable range (Mueller & Hancock, 2019; Dash & Paul, 2021;

Zaman et al., 2022).

PCTFL (H3).


Table 4 displays the research model H4, which suggests that the PS acts as a mediator between MOT and PCTFL.

The bootstrap approach was employed to identify significant mediating paths, with 95% confidence interval

estimates that exclude zero (MacKinnon et al., 2004;). Table 4 presents the direct and indirect effects of the

relationship between changes in MOT to PCTFL. The results indicate the direct influence of MOT on PCTFL was

significant. Furthermore, upon investigating the impact of PS (mediating variable), the connection between MOT

and PCTFL remained positive mediation. The estimated values (β = 0.405, t = 3.255, p < 0.001) revealed positive

mediation, suggesting that perceived skills significantly mediate the relationship between MOT and PCTFL (H4).

5. Discussion


This study is designed to integrate motivation, perceived skills and perceived changes in transformational

leadership style to understand the leadership training process that shape and influence perceived changes                in

transformational leadership. The hypothesis of this research posited that MOT has a positive impact on PCTFL

with the perceived skills playing a mediating role. The multidimensional (second-order factor structure

assessments) model conceptualized that MOT and PS positively affect the PCTFL.


H1. Motivation & perceived changes in transformational leadership style.


The results suggested that academics’ motivation for joining a leadership training programme (i.e., practical

enhancement, occupational promotion, and social contact) positively impacted the PCTFL, including idealized

influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, and individualized consideration, respectively. The

results are consistent with prior research by Trépanier et al. (2012) which found MOT has favourable effects on

reports of transformational leadership. This study provides novel evidence on the impact of MOT on PCTFL that

has not been previously theorized in the literature. Moreover, this finding extends previous findings that has

emphasized the crucial role of motivation in leadership, such as motivation and reports of transformational

leadership (Afshari et al., 2012); work motivations and outcomes of leadership (Kanat-Maymon et al., 2020),

motivation to lead on transformational leadership (Chan & Drasgow, 2001), motivation and leadership style

(Gilbert et al., 2016); motivational factors in leadership readiness (Boyce et al., 2010), as all of these built the

relationship between motivation and leadership. Additionally, previous findings show leadership training

positively impacts leaders’ behavioural means (Day & Sin, 2011; Kragt & Guenter, 2018). This study investigated

this relationship in a more detailed way, that is, motivation to join the leadership training and behavioural changes

in transformational leadership styles. This result also responds to Dinh et al.’s (2022) call, showing investigated

relationship between motivation in a leadership training programme and the behavioural changes after the training.

In addition, future research can also investigate the impact of other elements in the training on behavioural changes

in transformational leadership styles, such as, training contents and the role of training facilitators and instructors.

Altogether, the findings of present research contribute to the understanding of the positive impact of MOT on

PCTFL, providing valuable insights in this area of research.




H2. Motivation & perceived skills.


Additionally, the current study uncovered a significant finding regarding the influential role of MOT in eliciting

PS (H2). This suggests that the MOT (i.e., practical enhancement, occupational promotion, and social contact)

directly stimulated academic members’ perceived skills in leadership training. Motivationally driven and

metacognitively guided processing of the training skills is key for application (Brophy, 2010), and the training

skills are usually connected to prior knowledge (Goulet-Pelletier et al., 2023). Moreover, motivated academics

and potential leaders recognize the significance of their work and tend to view their activities as intrinsically

rewarding and opportunities for learning and growth (Løvaas et al., 2020). Consequently, having a better

understanding of the training content and higher-level of perceived skills. Although this new evidence on the

relationship between MOT and perceived skills has not been explicitly theorized in previous literature, it aligns

with earlier research that has highlighted the significant impact of motivation on performance (Løvaas et al., 2020)

and psychological growth (Douglas et al., 2020), as all of these prompted positive outcomes are from higher

motivations. Therefore, this specific finding represents a valuable contribution to the existing literature on the

relationship between motivation and perceived skills and highlights the importance of promoting motivation

among participants to enhance the effectiveness of leadership training programmes. This can also raise awareness

among training organizers of the crucial role that motivation plays in facilitating effective leadership development,

underscoring the need for tailored strategies to promote motivation among potential participants.




H3. Perceived skills & perceived changes in transformational leadership style.

Similarly, the present study examined the impact of perceived skills on PCTFL in the H3 relationship. The

findings from Mplus analysis indicated a significant positive relationship between perceived skills and PCTFL.

The finding highlighted the role of perceived skills in the leadership training in enhancing the perceived changes

in transformational leadership in terms of idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation,

and individualized consideration. This novel finding of the positive impact of perceived skills on PCTFL is

noteworthy, as it was rarely measured previously. It is intriguing to compare this result with findings from previous

studies on leadership training programmes. Previous work of leadership skills has reflected effective in building

fellows’ self-efficacy (Fernandez et al., 2022). Moreover, technical skills have a significant relationship with

transformational leadership role (Afshari et al., 2012), adding to the relevance of the findings in this study.

Moreover, existing research has advocated the relationship between the skills approach and transformational

leadership (Megheirkouni et al., 2018), aligning with the results of this study that highlight the positive impact of

perceived skills on PCTFL. Additionally, previous research (Dinh et al., 2022; Merino-Soto et al., 2022)

highlighted that exploring learners’ perception of skills is essential as it closely relates to behavioural actions, this

finding provides evidence for this close relationship.

H4. Perceived skills positively mediate the relationship between motivation and perceived changes in

transformational leadership style


The results of H4 analysis, which examined the mediating role of PS in the relationship between MOT and

PCTFL, demonstrated a significant positive mediation. This suggests that MOT factor plays a crucial role in

fostering enthusiasm and engagement among the training participants, thereby they tend to perform well and have a

good understanding and knowledge of the training content and enhanced learning. This consequently led to perceived

enhanced skills, which favourably impacted PCTFL. The results of this study, which highlight the mediating role

of PS in a positive relationship between MOT and PCTFL, align with earlier research findings, where training

design (Nafukho et al., 2022) has been identified as a mediating role in the relationship between the trainee’s

learning-oriented motivation and the transfer of learning. Additionally, positive effect, which encompasses

pleasant emotions, has been found to mediate the relationship between intrinsic motivation and skill development

(Kaše et al., 2019). The mediating role of PS between the MOT and PCTFL in this research adds to the novelty

of the findings. Further research can be conducted to explore the specific aspects of the mediating role of perceived

skills and whether it differs based on the type of leadership training or the characteristics of the participants.




6. Limitation and recommendations for further research

There are a few limitations to be noted in this study. First, other potential variables may influence investigated

relationships and their mediating effects, such as gender, types of training, age, and work-related leadership

experience, training content, and the role of training facilitators and instructors etc. Future research can be

conducted to investigate this. Second, in addition to quantitative method, qualitative approach can be used to

provide in-depth insights into the complex dynamics between motivation and PCTFL in various dimensions, we

can work on perceptions of learners toward their changes in transformational leadership after joining the training,

etc. For instance, qualitative research questions such as “What training content brings the most behavioural

changes as perceived by the participants and how?” etc. can be addressed. By employing a mixed-methods

approach, researchers can gain a more holistic understanding of the phenomenon under investigation.

7. Conclusion and implication




In this study, we obtained results that motivation for joining leadership training programmes encourages PCTFL,

supporting the idea that transformational leadership can be developed (Bass & Riggio, 2006). By including

participants’ perceived skills, which transmit leadership skills indicating approaches that are conducive to

perceived changes in leadership behaviours. Therefore, the results indicate that transformational leadership can

be is developed when participants are motivated for leadership training and also when they perceive learning gains

in perceived skills.


Our study makes a contribution to the transformation leadership literature by proposing and empirically testing a

novel model in which transformational leadership is enhanced by participants’ motivation through the mediation

of perceived skills. This suggests that motivation has a positive impact on perceived changes in transformational

leadership styles, implying that future leadership theories should include motivation as a key component in

understanding how leaders develop and adapt their leadership styles. Leadership theories may need to explore

how leaders’ personal motivation contributes to their ongoing progress and development as effective educational

leaders, and could delve into the role of self-belief and confidence in one’s abilities as a driving force behind the

development of transformational leadership.


Our study also provides practical implications for leadership development programmes, leadership training

organisers may well design and evaluate programmes on leadership training to effectively pass on the knowledge

of transformational leadership factors to future leaders. Leadership training programmes can focus on enhancing

these factors to improve leadership training effectiveness. For example, leadership training programmes could

include activities that promote motivation, such as goal-setting and rewards systems. They could also include

competency-based training programmes that focus on developing the skills and abilities of participants to enhance

transformational leadership. These training programmes could improve the leadership effectiveness of academic

members and educational leaders to enhance the quality of education for students.
