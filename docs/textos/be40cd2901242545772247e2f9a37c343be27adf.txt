                                                       Abstract
This study explores effective models of principal identity in relation to school management and
leadership within the uMgungundlovu District of KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. It examines how
principals perceive and develop their professional identities, and how these evolving identities impact
effective school management and administration. Therefore, the study identifies both the challenges and
potential solutions for improving school leadership practices. The central argument is that a principal's
influence and effectiveness stem from the continuous evolution of their professional identity from their
experiences as teachers to their eventual roles as school leaders. Grounded in the theory of
intersectionality, as a lens to uncover the identity of principals for effective management. The study
employs an interpretivist qualitative paradigm to explain principals’ identities. Data was collected
through semi-structured interviews, focus group discussions, and document analysis. Thematic
analysis was used to interpret emerging themes and provide nuanced insights into the participants’
experiences. Findings reveal that understanding a principal’s identity is fundamental to enhancing
school management and leadership effectiveness. The study concludes that collaboration and open
communication among principals are critical to organizational success. Furthermore, sharing
professional experiences, resources, and best practices fosters a deeper appreciation of how identity
development contributes to effective school leadership.
Keyword: Principal’s identity; identity models, effective school management; collaboration;
organisational success
1. INTRODUCTION

teammate, coach, or therapist when the need arises. (Supovitz, 2019). It takes a wide range of
skills to lead through change, including non-behavioural, non-practice-related leadership
elements that have an influence on leadership behaviour and practices. Principals in South
Africa were just collaborators during the Apartheid era, but since the country's schools
transitioned to democracy, principals have played a crucial role in students' academic success
and the overall success of the school (Mestry, 2017). Also, principals serve as key managers in

School' heads, according to Lara (2018), lead the schools in fulfilling the school's goal, vision,

1.1.     Literature Review
1.1.1.   Identity Construction
         According to Scribner et al. (2011), it is helpful to understand how identity affects
leadership styles by determining how school principals create their identities. School
principals' identity construction is also influenced by professional expectations (Crow et al.,
2016). According to some researchers, a person's professional identity evolves but becomes a
permanent characteristic once internalised (DeRue et al., 2009). Additionally, according to
Crow et al. (2016), school principals construct their identities through their background, the
environments of their institutions, and the relationships they form with stakeholders.

         Teachers go through a transformation process in the classroom when they develop a
leadership identity. The shift from a focus on teaching and learning to assuming an
administrative and leadership role can lead to challenges for some educators as they navigate
their evolving identities. This transition from teacher to principal may result in difficulties
regarding their self-perception (Browne-Ferrigno, 2013). Some teachers learn their new
identities on the job, while others have to undergo formal training. This suggests that identity
construction may occur through reflection on the professional development process. Moorosi
(2013) reports that participants in a principal development program in South Africa were able
to assess how their self-confidence and self-control had improved at the end of the program.

1.1.2.   Leadership Attributes

         Research has shown that the personal qualities of school principals are linked to
effective school leadership. Personal qualities that influence school principals' leadership
identities include listening comprehension, empathy, trust, patience, and stress management,
among others (Saarukka, 2014). The characteristics of an effective school principal, as outlined
by Crow et al. (2016), encompass accountability, emotional intelligence, and dedication.
According to the participants in a study by Dös and Savas (2015), good leadership traits
include tolerance, hard work, objectivity in decision-making, motivational skills, and a good
sense of humour. Moorosi (2013) distinguishes leadership qualities based on gender.

According to the study, among other things, the male principals were more authoritative, had
a clear vision, and set an example, whereas the female principals were more approachable,
transparent, had good cooperation skills, and were willing to consult.

         Even though some traits of both men and women have been linked to effective
leadership, Goldring et al. (2007) contend that personal characteristics do not appear to
significantly impact principals' work, and they recommend that more research should be
conducted to investigate this relationship. According to their research, contextual factors have
a greater impact on the school principals' time allocation for the various tasks than their
personal characteristics.

1.1.3.   Roles and Responsibilities of School Principals

         The role of school principals is described as complex, multifaceted, and dynamic in
the literature. They are expected to take on various roles when interacting with teachers and
students, such as being a teacher, a leader, and a parental figure. School principals play
essential roles in establishing a vision, fostering growth, restructuring the organization, and
overseeing teaching and learning processes. However, due to time constraints, they often
prioritize administrative tasks over instructional leadership. Principals in different countries,
such as Turkey and South Africa, have varying priorities and challenges in fulfilling their
responsibilities. Research suggests that principals need to balance managerial duties with
instructional leadership to be effective leaders. The study aims to explore how principals'
perceptions of their leadership identities influence their prioritization and execution of their
roles, considering the diverse responsibilities they are expected to manage.

1.1.4 Effective School Management and Leadership

         Effective school management involves maintaining optimal practices that produce the
most favourable outcomes in the administration of education and the facilitation of teaching
and learning (Day, Sammons, & Gorgen, 2020). The management of a school encompasses the
regulation of various activities aimed at achieving specific objectives (Westbrook et al., 2013).
Effective school management indicates that the administration is capable of attaining the
intended results. Furthermore, the caliber of school leadership can significantly impact the
quality of educators, teaching methods, and overall outcomes (Mestry, 2017). Leadership and
management are linked and are equally necessary if schools are to be effective and efficient,
and lead to school improvement (Mestry, 2017). The school principal collaborates with the

school management team (SMT) to guarantee the success of the institution. School leadership
has a significant indirect impact on student learning outcomes based on their role in effective
school management (Leithwood et al., 2020).

       Ramrathan (2017) states that school leadership constitutes a practice that fosters
positive influence, growth, and development for both individuals and groups in pursuit of
collective objectives. Within the educational sphere, leadership is shaped by global and
societal trends and pressures. Researchers have sought to analyze leadership from the
perspective of schools (Hallinger & Chen, 2015). School leaders occupy a pivotal role in a
complex environment that necessitates their possession of relevant knowledge and skills (Yan
& Ehrich, 2009). Pathak et al. (2011) argue that school leaders are expected to guide, inspire,
and direct members of the school community, ensuring that they understand educational
goals and the means available to achieve them.

       Fairman and Mackenzie (2015) assert that effective school leadership highlights the
significance of a unified vision within the school community, as well as the importance of
collaborative efforts and professional feedback in fostering changes in attitudes, motivation,
and behaviour. The foundation of school leadership is rooted in a shared vision, which
depends on the dedication and reciprocal support of the school community, ultimately
leading to enhanced educational results. Consequently, the role of school leadership is vital
in advancing school performance (Brescoll, 2016).

       The school principal plays a variety of roles essential for the effective operation of the
institution, as noted by Meador (2019). These responsibilities encompass maintaining student
discipline, evaluating teachers, developing new policies, revising existing ones, securing
funding, and managing the school budget, among other tasks. Nevertheless, the principal's
primary responsibility is to provide leadership. Contemporary school leaders are required to
possess a comprehensive understanding of finance, curriculum development, child
psychology, human resource management, time management, community engagement,
public relations, and exceptional communication abilities (Alkaabi, et al. 2022).

       Leadership effectiveness is typically understood as the ability of leaders to inspire and
mobilize their followers in a manner that promotes the objectives of both the group and the
organization. A fundamental aspect of this understanding is that leadership focuses on
achieving collective goals rather than individual aspirations (Steffens, et al. 2021).
Furthermore, leadership occurs within the framework of shared group membership, between

leaders and their followers, which influences how followers react to their leader; thus, the
effectiveness of leadership is shaped by this narrative (Epitropaki et al. 2017). Effective school

effective teaching and learning. They recognise outstanding achievements in order to push
employees to strive in all aspects of their jobs (Peterson, 2013). Therefore, principals
understand their identities in relation to effective school management and leadership impacts
on teachers, learners, and the broader school community.

1.2.   Theoretical Framework

       Intersectionality theory, developed by Kimberlé Crenshaw in the late 1980s and later
expanded by Patricia Hill Collins, was initially used to explain the experiences of Black
women who faced overlapping forms of discrimination based on race and gender. Over time,
the theory has evolved to encompass the complex and interconnected nature of multiple
identity categories, such as race, gender, class, and professional role, that shape individuals’
experiences across social and institutional contexts. The central premise of intersectionality is
that identity is multifaceted and that individuals occupy multiple social positions that
intersect to influence their perceptions, opportunities, and actions.

       In the context of educational leadership, intersectionality provides a valuable
framework for understanding how principals’ various identities, such as professional,
personal, cultural, and social, interact to shape their leadership styles, decision-making, and
management practices. It illuminates how institutional structures and power relations
influence principals' experiences and how they negotiate their roles within schools. By
acknowledging the intersection of these identities, the theory helps to uncover the nuanced
challenges and privileges that principals encounter in their pursuit of effective school
management and leadership.

       Intersectionality also deepens our understanding of the relationships between identity
and power within school settings. It reveals how societal hierarchies and organizational
norms affect leadership development and the enactment of authority. While the theory’s early
focus on Black women has drawn criticism for limiting its scope, contemporary scholars have
refined it to address broader and more diverse professional contexts, including educational
leadership. Researchers have thus employed intersectionality to examine how multiple
identity dimensions, such as gender, ethnicity, and professional role, converge to influence
leadership behaviour and institutional outcomes.

                       Andalas Management Review, Vol. 9 No. 2, 2025


       Within this study, intersectionality serves as a theoretical lens for exploring how
principals construct and perform their professional identities in relation to effective school
management. It offers insight into how principals’ overlapping identities are shaped by their
personal histories, social positions, and institutional roles that affect their leadership practices
and responses to challenges. By applying this theory, the study situates principal identity not
as a fixed attribute but as a dynamic and evolving construct that emerges through continuous
interaction between the individual and the institutional environment. In this way,
intersectionality provides a robust framework for analysing the complexities of principal
identity models and their implications for effective school management and leadership.

2. METHOD
       A qualitative research approach was adopted to explore effective models of principal
identity in relation to school management and leadership within the uMgungundlovu District
of KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. This approach was chosen for its ability to generate rich, in-
depth descriptions and nuanced insights into the lived experiences of participants. By
focusing on meaning rather than measurement, qualitative inquiry enabled the researcher to
capture the complexity of principals’ leadership identities as they evolved within their
professional and institutional contexts. This approach was particularly suitable for examining
how principals interpret their roles, navigate challenges, and enact leadership practices that
shape effective school management.

       The study was guided by an interpretivist paradigm, which emphasizes
understanding the subjective meanings individuals construct through their experiences. From
this perspective, reality is viewed as socially constructed and best understood through the
voices of those directly involved. In alignment with Creswell’s (2009) qualitative data analysis
framework, data collection involved semi-structured interviews and document analysis to
explore principals’ perspectives and professional narratives. The analysis process entailed
organizing, coding, and interpreting the data to uncover recurring themes and patterns
related to identity construction and leadership practice. Through this interpretive lens, the
study sought to illuminate how principals’ evolving identities influence their management
decisions and contribute to the overall effectiveness of their schools.

       Sampling is the process of choosing a group of participants for research in a way that
ensures they are representative of the wider group from which they were chosen (Smith,
2012). The researchers employed the purposive sampling technique to choose a group of


                                                                                                151

                       Andalas Management Review, Vol. 9 No. 2, 2025


participants for this study (Smith, 2012). Purposive sampling allowed the researcher to select
participants most likely to provide rich information relevant to the research objectives (Etikan
et al., 2016). Additionally, purposive sampling allowed the researcher to select participants
with the knowledge and experience most relevant to the research questions (Hussain et al.,
2018). Given the in-depth nature of this study, which aimed to explore school principals’
identity model construction through interviews and observations, a sample of six primary
schools from Umgungundlovu District was chosen.

       This approach facilitated focused data collection within each school context. One
principal was selected per school, resulting in a total of six principals participating in
individual interviews. Also, focus groups were formulated by three teachers, namely T1, T2,
and T3. While the sample size (six schools) represented a small percentage of the total
population (335 schools), purposive sampling prioritised information-rich data over statistical
generalisability. The focus of this research was to gain deep insights into principals' identity
construction through in-depth interviews and document analysis. Limiting the number of
schools allows more intensive exploration within each selected context.

       This study employed a combination of three data collection methods to gain a
comprehensive understanding of the research question, which includes one-on-one
interviews, document analysis, and focus group interviews. While each method offered
unique strengths, a triangulation approach (Flick, 2018) was used to corroborate and enrich
the data, leading to a more robust understanding of the phenomenon under investigation.

    3.1. Theme 1: Consultation with other Stakeholders

         The significance of the principal's consultation and involvement with stakeholders and
the community at large is explored in this theme. A school's stakeholders are the learners
themselves, families and guardians, teachers, administrators, custodians, secretaries, and
other school staff. It is evident from the responses of the research participants that engaging
in the community is essential for school success. Building partnerships with parents and other
stakeholders can enhance school support and progress. It is of utmost importance that
principals should foster a collaborative leadership style that involves staff members and
stakeholders in the decision-making processes of school administration. Participant (P4) made
a simple summary of this when she noted that:

“All stakeholders need to work together to prepare learners for their future. The task is immense, but
learning outcomes can be surpassed while fostering essential skills if we work in unison. Given strong
sources of support, learners will achieve at higher levels, develop crucial social-emotional skills, build
leadership capacity, and become more autonomous in their future learning” (P4).

         This approach encourages a sense of ownership and responsibility among
stakeholders, leading to a more engaged and motivated workforce. When staff members are
involved in the decision-making process, they feel a sense of ownership over the school's
direction, leading to increased commitment and improved school performance (Bryman et al.,
2018). Shared leadership and collaboration are essential components of effective school
management. By empowering staff, fostering a collaborative culture, and valuing diverse
perspectives, principals can create a positive and productive school environment. Research
participant (P3) elaborated on the need for a collaborative effort from the principals and
stakeholders for school success. He noted that:

a short-term or long-term basis and give clear steps for achieving them. It is about acquiring

workforce, which is the education system in this case. This theme investigates the ongoing
professional development activities in schools engineered by the school principals to ensure
continuous positive output at the schools they are leading and managing. It is important to
note that school principals exercise significant influence on teacher professional development.

       Based on this, four areas were identified where principals could have a substantial
impact on the teacher's continuous learning process. These include the principal as an
instructional leader and learner, the creation of a learning environment, direct involvement in
the design, delivery, and content of professional development, and finally, the assessment of
professional development outcomes.

“Change in education is driven by teachers, but teachers have been left out of the conversation. They

        Teachers need to be interested and engaged. Just like their learners, teachers learn in

methods. The success of a professional development activity relies not only on its immediate
impact but also on its longevity in the classroom.

        A systematic review published in 2021 indicates a strong correlation between the
quality of teaching and student outcomes, as well as educational equity. Notably, the review
highlights that specific types of professional development are linked to substantial and lasting
improvements in both teaching practices and student performance. In the absence of ongoing
learning and support, educators may be reluctant to adopt new strategies and concepts in
their classrooms, potentially resulting in inefficient utilization of time and resources.
Consequently, it is essential to ensure that teachers are provided with adequate support for
their professional development as they work to improve their instructional methods.

        Thus, for new teachers, having a mentor in the school could help them through
common teaching challenges like preparing lesson plans and mastering classroom
management. For in-service teachers, mentoring and participation in capacity-building
activities offer them valuable learning opportunities to lead and drive change in their school
and career. Teachers can also connect with other educators in their district or even participate
in an exchange program during the school year, so mentoring doesn't have to take place at the
same school. In short, professional development activities could take the form of classes,
activities, and training that focus on improving teacher instruction, achieving mastery in
instructional planning, and bringing about a positive academic performance in the school.
Therefore, it is critical for the school principal to have well-rounded and developed staff that
can assist the principal to work with a strong team in trying to achieve a common goal, which
is aligned with the vision and mission of the school.

    3.3. Theme 3: Staff meetings and communication

        Staff meetings can be a vital avenue for effective communication. Effective
communication and collaboration are essential for school success, and staff meetings have
been a major source of fostering this necessity. The findings of this study highlight the critical
role of staff meetings and open communication channels in fostering a positive and

                        Andalas Management Review, Vol. 9 No. 2, 2025


productive school environment. Staff meetings can help keep employees informed, promote
communication, and encourage feedback.

        Principals emphasised the importance of regular staff meetings as a platform for
information sharing, decision-making, and building a shared sense of purpose. Participant
(P3) noted the importance of staff meetings by stating that:

"Meetings, if done properly, are the most effective way of making decisions and motivating staff. In
effective meetings, participants can contribute and share their thoughts and, as a result, feel more
engaged in the school. If the discussion is properly documented in the minutes of the meeting and a
comprehensive tasks list, the meetings always lead to tangible results. Teachers are free to raise their
voices in our meetings. I believe that everyone has a right to express a view, and my leadership does not
revolve around the principal, as the last speaker was saying." (P3).

Participant (P1) places much emphasis on the need to have a stipulated agenda before the
meeting, and according to her, this is the only way to get the staff properly engaged during
the meeting.

"A meeting without an agenda is like going to an exam without studying, a waste of time. I make sure
that all participants receive the agenda, including any supporting material, at least 48 hours before the
meeting, so they can prepare and add any topics they believe need to be discussed. When everyone is
properly prepared, the actual meeting will be much easier, but you, as a leader, must manage the
discussion and make sure it stays on track. Start on time, end on time. Summarize at the end of each
topic and document all decisions and action items" (P1).

        Open communication channels are vital for fostering a collaborative school culture.
Regular staff meetings, as well as informal opportunities for interaction, contribute to a sense

research participants (P2) noted:

"We hold staff meetings, and we have general staff meetings as well; we also hold urgent staff meetings,
if need be. Also, we do hold extraordinary meetings with both SMT and staff. There is room for all
participants to raise their concerns and make input during the meetings. As I have said, I lead with
people. I do not regard staff members as objects because they have their own minds, and we all share the
same objective. I keep all the records of such meetings in the form of minutes" (P2).

        The need for active involvement of staff during the meetings was also observed by
another participant (P5), who noted that:

1. Understanding Personal Identities

        According to Aguilar (2012), personal identity is the concept of who a person is, which
includes their beliefs, personalities, experiences, and name; it is a consistent aspect of a
person's life that develops over time. Personal identity helps people to understand themselves
and how they differ from others. Consequently, from the findings, personal identity helps
determine effective school management and leadership. The principal's leadership style plays
a significant role in curriculum delivery and has a significant impact on learner outcomes
(Lindberg, 2014). Schools' heads, according to Lara (2018), lead the schools in fulfilling the
schools' goals, vision, and strategic plans. Their identities have a big influence on this: they
determine the schools' identities. They keep thinking about this identity because of how well
it has performed in terms of learner results. They continually consider this identity,
considering its demonstrated effectiveness in improving learner results (Whiteman, Scribner,
& Crow, 2015).




                                                                                            162

                        Andalas Management Review, Vol. 9 No. 2, 2025


         Personal identity is helpful in comprehending how principals make meaning of their
duties and situate themselves according to the requirements of the school environment (Burke
& Stets, 2009). According to Whiteman, Scribner and Crow (2015), identity context can
introduce vulnerabilities that complicate the job of the principal in various ways. The findings
of this study indicate that the principals' source of influence is their identities. According to
Ibarra and Barbulescu (2010), identity is built and reshaped as people change their personal
narratives about themselves, as well as the narrative types they discuss and draw upon when
creating descriptions or identity narratives. Smulyan (2000) stipulated that there are four
types of contexts where identities fall. In the first context, the principals operate in a personal
context. This context consists of their family and educational background. The second context
is community, which comprises the school environment. The third context is positions of
power and structural regularities that influence the principals’ actions. The last context is the
historical and social context, which consists of hierarchies’ norms and patterns (Smulyan,
2000).

         The findings of the study focused on the second and third contexts, and they examined
how principals create, maintain, and constantly deconstruct their identities, as well as how
identity affects their leadership. It was found that school principals have different
perspectives on how they perform their duties and how they see their roles in schools and
society. Thus, principals must understand their personal identities so that their leadership and
management are not influenced by their personalities. Understanding personal identities is
crucial for this study as it helps to unpack the research questions and to determine the
principals' identities in terms of effective school management and leadership. Understanding
personal identities will also help to ascertain if principals understand their identities as a
component of effective school management and leadership, as stipulated in the research
objective.

2. Instructional Leadership

         Instructional leadership is concerned with leadership for learning as much as it is with
supporting learning and learning outcomes, guaranteeing quality in teaching, and improving
school and learner results (Zheng, Yin & Li 2019). This is leadership that pays peculiar
attention to teaching and learning. From the research findings, principals as instructional
leaders pay attention to classroom learning activities. Instructional leadership is generally
perceived to be focused on achievement standards, accountability, and school reform. The
primary responsibility of instructional leadership is to build and influence the school culture

                                                                                               163

                      Andalas Management Review, Vol. 9 No. 2, 2025


to improve teaching and learning (Ohlson, 2009). The research findings indicated that
instructional leadership is first associated with improving learning and learner performance.
The research has equally established how instructional leadership impacts learners' overall
achievement and school management.

       Instructional leaders are tasked with a range of critical responsibilities, which
encompass advocating, planning, implementation, supporting, communicating, evaluating,
and monitoring, in addition to interpreting the curriculum and planning for school
improvement (Wang, 2016). Consequently, instructional leadership is characterized by a focus
on motivating followers to enhance teaching and learning, rather than merely overseeing the
daily operations of the school (Bush, 2008). In essence, principals acting as instructional
leaders take a comprehensive approach to all school activities, actively engaging with both
staff and students in their pursuit of success. They champion more effective teaching and
learning practices and prioritize instructional needs, rather than function solely as school
managers. From the perspective of instructional leadership, the research objective concerning
the impact of principals' identities on their management and leadership roles has been
successfully addressed. This has facilitated the exploration of pertinent research questions,
particularly regarding the proposed model that principals could adopt for aligning their
identities for the sake of promoting effective school management and leadership.

3. Individual Support

       Leadership, according to Pekkolay (2021), is the ability to urge and inspire others to
undertake things they would not typically consider. Therefore, for effective school leadership
and management, there is a need for continuous support for school leaders and teachers. The
individual support for school principals can include professional development opportunities,
which are interventions for principals to improve themselves through learning and training.
Principals require ongoing support to balance the dictates of their identity and leadership
demands, especially when it comes to implementing difficult school policies. By providing
principals with resources, strategies, and guidance, these forms of support would help them
to effectively plan and prioritise their approach, activities, and engagements. Providing access
to counseling services, stress management programs, and strategies for work-life balance can
help principals manage leadership pressures and stress.




4. Participative Leadership

                                                                                            164

                      Andalas Management Review, Vol. 9 No. 2, 2025


       Participative leadership, equally known as democratic leadership and shared
leadership, entails leadership and management styles that involve the employees in the
decision-making process of the institution or organisation. In participative leadership, the
leader delegates the responsibility, collects opinions, reviews feedback, and guides the team
to a final decision. Participative leadership has goals of improving decision-making,
enhancing employees' sense of ownership of their work and integrating employees' personal
goals with the organisation's goals (Yan & Ehrich, 2009). Ramrathan (2017) defines school
leadership as a practice that leads to positive influence, growth, and development of both the
individuals and groups for a collective purpose and common goal.

       From the perspective of this leadership style, the principal and the School
Management Team (SMT) should work together to ensure the school’s success. To achieve
this purpose, the school principal must master democratic leadership skills and principles to
carry everyone along. This style of leadership makes room for more people to participate in
the decision-making process. Besides, participative leadership helps to build trust by
demonstrating the importance and value of group members' opinions on important matters,
and it significantly benefits organisations by fostering a collaborative and innovative
environment. A participative leader considers the perspective of everyone in the team and
encourages employees to be heavily involved in joint decision-making (Brescoll, 2016).
Participative leadership could motivate all staff members, as they feel important through their
involvement in decision-making for the common goal of the school, which includes the
mission and vision of the school.

5. Effective Management and Leadership

       Leadership and management are linked and equally necessary if schools are to be
effective, efficient, and geared towards school improvement (Mestry, 2017). According to Day,
Sammons and Gorgen (2020), effective school management relates to sustaining the best
practices that yield the best results in school management and administration of teaching and
learning. When school management is effective, it implies that the school administration can
achieve the desired results. The principal and the SMT work together to ensure the school’s
success. School leadership has a significant indirect impact on learners’ learning outcomes
based on their role in effective school management (Leithwood et al., 2020).

       Fairman and Mackenzie (2015) defined leadership effectiveness as the ability of
leaders to inspire and mobilize their followers in ways that promote the objectives of both the


                                                                                           165

                      Andalas Management Review, Vol. 9 No. 2, 2025


group and the organization. Effective school principals are characterized by their ability to
provide clear guidance, motivate their staff, and create an environment conducive to
successful teaching and learning. Furthermore, school leadership highlights the importance
of a shared vision within the school community, as well as the significance of collaborative
efforts and professional feedback in fostering changes in attitudes, motivation, and behaviour.
This form of leadership is fundamentally anchored in a collective vision and depends on the
dedication and mutual support of the school community, which can lead to enhanced
educational outcomes. A crucial aspect of this perspective is that leadership focuses on
achieving collective goals rather than individual ambitions (Steffens, Munt, & van
Knippenberg, 2021). In summary, effective leadership by principals is characterized by the
encouragement of collaborative initiatives and the maintenance of a positive working
environment within the school. Through this approach, the principal collaborates with the
staff to establish a shared vision, enhance academic performance, and cultivate a leadership
culture that engages all members of the staff.

4. CONCLUSION AND IMPLICATIONS
       The study proposes a conceptual model designed to understand how principals’
identities influence effective school management and leadership. The model consists of five
interrelated components: understanding personal identity, instructional leadership,
individual support, participative leadership, and effective management and leadership. These
components collectively highlight the dynamic relationship between a principal’s self-concept
and their professional actions, demonstrating how identity shapes leadership behaviour,
decision-making, and school culture.

       The study concludes that effective school management within the uMgungundlovu
District is closely linked to the adoption and integration of principals' identity models. It
recommends that collaboration and communication among principals within the same district
or geographical area should be actively encouraged to foster the sharing of resources, best
practices, and professional experiences. Such collaboration can enhance both novice and
experienced principals' understanding of how identity influences leadership effectiveness.
Furthermore, future research should focus on applying and refining this identity-based model
in diverse educational contexts to advance the continuous improvement of school
management and leadership practices.
