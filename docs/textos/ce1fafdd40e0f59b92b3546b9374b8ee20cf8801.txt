Abstract
This article addresses a significant gap in the literature by synthesizing peer-reviewed
evidence on artificial intelligence (AI) applications in school management, focusing
on administrative decision-making, resource allocation, operational support, and
educational leadership. The review follows PRISMA guidelines and draws on
approximately forty peer-reviewed studies published between 2018 and 2026,
retrieved from SpringerLink, Sage Journals, ScienceDirect, ERIC, and Google
Scholar. To contextualize AI-enabled administrative functions, the analysis also
incorporates research from organizational studies, management information systems,
business process management, and cybersecurity. Particular attention is given to
explainable AI and stewardship, including formal modeling approaches for
administrative processes and techniques that enhance the transparency and
accountability of algorithmic decision-making. The review further examines financial
management, student data protection, cybersecurity, interoperability, federated
learning, and the integration of large language models into school information and
ERP systems. Three key themes emerge. First, AI can improve administrative
efficiency, reduce workload, strengthen data-informed decision-making, and optimize
organizational processes. Second, successful implementation depends on robust
governance frameworks, ethical guidelines, AI literacy among staff and leaders,
effective data protection and cybersecurity measures, interoperability planning, and
adequate digital infrastructure. Third, core dimensions of educational leadership—
including professional judgment, relational trust, contextual understanding, creativity,
and ethical accountability—remain indispensable and should be preserved as AI
adoption expands. Based on these findings, the article proposes three research
hypotheses concerning the relationship between AI-driven automation and
administrative workload, the role of AI governance frameworks in shaping
stakeholder outcomes, and the moderating effect of organizational support on the
relationship between AI integration and institutional performance. The review also
identifies methodological limitations in the existing evidence base, which remains
dominated by conceptual and qualitative studies. Overall, AI offers substantial
potential to transform school management, but its effectiveness depends on sustained

Introduction
        School leaders in the twenty-first century operate in an
increasingly complex environment. Principals, district administrators,
and school management teams are simultaneously expected to drive
instructional improvement, manage increasingly diverse student
populations, navigate evolving legislative and regulatory requirements,
satisfy stakeholder accountability demands, and maintain operational
efficiency within constrained financial resources. Administrative tasks
— scheduling, attendance monitoring, budget management, reporting,
staff appraisal, resource allocation, parent and community
communication — consume enormous proportions of school leaders'
time and cognitive bandwidth, often at the direct expense of
instructional leadership, professional development, and the relational
work that animates healthy school cultures.
         Into this context, artificial intelligence has arrived not as a
speculative futuristic prospect, but as an increasingly present
technological reality. Machine learning algorithms, natural language
processing systems, predictive analytics platforms, computer vision
tools, and decision-support architectures are already being piloted and
deployed across educational settings worldwide. Vendors market AI-
powered school management systems that claim to automate routine
administrative tasks, generate real-time performance dashboards,
optimize staff scheduling, predict student attendance and dropout risk,
and provide evidence-based recommendations to school leaders.
National and regional education authorities in several countries have
begun developing AI policy frameworks for schools. At the same time,
critical scholars have raised urgent questions about algorithmic bias,
data privacy, the displacement of human professional judgment, and the
risk that AI adoption in schools may deepen rather than mitigate

existing educational inequities. Across organizational contexts more
broadly, AI adoption in management information systems (MIS) and
management research has accelerated, yet reviews identify persistent
gaps in understanding key adoption factors, topic evolution, and the
socio-technical relationship between artificial and human intelligence
(Islami & Mulolli, 2024, p.4; Liu et al., 2024, p.3; Stoykova & Shakev,
2023, p.9). These developments raise questions about how to design,
deploy, and govern AI so that it remains explainable, context-sensitive,
and aligned with human educational and organizational goals.
Explainability represents a central concern across these domains. In
compulsory education, prediction systems that estimate pupils' future
attendance rely on private data and can affect access to schooling and
grades, thereby risking disadvantage for underperforming students if
their operation remains opaque (Kammüller & Satija, 2023, p.8). In
adjacent domains, deep AI networks trained on large benchmark
datasets are being repurposed for tasks such as waste classification,
where object contamination and deformation challenge generalizability
and highlight the need for domain-informed augmentation and
explanation of model behavior; kernel-weight analyses of architectures
such as ResNet, ConvNeXt, and vision transformers show diminishing
contributions from deeper layers, motivating leaner architectures that
balance accuracy with explainability (Arashpour, 2023) — a tension
that maps directly onto the trade-off between predictive power and
interpretability that recurs throughout the school-management
literature. In educational leadership specifically, analyses of Principal
Ethical AI Leadership (PEAIL) portray a conceptually rich but
empirically preliminary field, in which advocacy for values such as
fairness and inclusion outpaces systematic evidence about how leaders
handle day-to-day ethical dilemmas around AI (Ho, 2026). This
scholarship further notes an under-representation of principals' own
voices, uneven contextual diversity, and a narrow methodological
repertoire, all of which constrain the development of robust, context-
sensitive guidance for school leaders navigating AI-related decisions.
Formal modeling and systematic review methodologies have been
proposed to address different facets of these challenges. In the case of
pupils' attendance prediction, the Isabelle Infrastructure Framework

concerned not with AI as a tool for teaching and learning, but with AI
as an instrument of school organization, management, operational
support, and leadership stewardship. The guiding question is: what does
the current evidence base — spanning education-specific research,
ethical-leadership scholarship, and adjacent organizational,
management-information-systems, business-process-management, and
cybersecurity literatures — tell us about how AI can be integrated into
school management and support processes, and what are the conditions,
benefits, risks, explainability requirements, and implications of such
integration for educational leadership?
   1. Background and Conceptual Foundations

       1.1. Dimensions of AI in Educational Contexts

        Within modern schooling systems, AI-driven tools represent a
new phase in this evolution by offering data-driven personalization and
automation within large-scale settings. Operationalizations of AI in K–
12 contexts include predictive analytics that forecast academic
performance or behavioral risk, adaptive learning environments,
intelligent tutoring systems that personalize instruction and feedback,
and generative models such as ChatGPT. Research on AI in education

conceptualizes these applications as a heterogeneous umbrella of
machine-learning and data-driven systems embedded in everyday
school practice, extending the longstanding project of managing
diversity and individual needs within mass education. Systematic
reviews of Principal Ethical AI Leadership use this operational
definition to examine how school leaders confront equity, inclusion,
and accountability issues arising from the deployment of such systems
(Ho, 2026). Insights from the adjacent literature on AI applied to
business process management indicate that machine learning and
natural language processing can be employed to monitor and predict
process trajectories while also surfacing practical problems such as
label ambiguity and limited generalization — issues directly relevant
when school systems deploy predictive analytics and adaptive tutoring
tools (Fettke & Di Francescomarino, 2025, p.69). Label ambiguity,
noisy event acquisition, and weak generalization often limit reliability
and can make explanations brittle in practice, with similar data issues
potentially eroding fairness and producing opaque recommendations
when adaptive tutors or risk models are deployed across diverse student
populations. Business process management research also explores
techniques for improving event-log quality and semantically enriching
process descriptions, measures that may suggest practical ways to
strengthen prediction and explainability when analogous analytics are
introduced in educational systems (Fettke & Di Francescomarino, 2025,
p.73). A complementary strand centers on AI literacy and curriculum
design. Contemporary analyses propose that integrating AI into
education proceeds along two intertwined pathways: preparing students
to participate in AI-shaped societies and economies, and deploying AI
itself to support and personalize learning environments. The first
pathway involves deliberately embedding topics such as coding,
machine learning, data science, robotics, and neural networks across
educational stages, beginning with foundational exposure in early
schooling and advancing into higher education. The second entails
engaging learners directly with AI tools and platforms, including large
language models, AI-powered textbooks, and other modalities that
personalize content and interaction. Historical experience with
curriculum reform informs current calls for comprehensive competency

frameworks that move beyond technical skills to include critical
thinking, problem solving, and reflection on ethical issues such as data
privacy, algorithmic bias, and broader societal impacts. Efforts such as
the Council of Europe's proposed “compass for AI in education,”

concrete guidance for responsible, human-centric AI use in education,
exemplify this institutionalization of AI-related competencies within a
long tradition of policy frameworks steering educational change
(Reimers et al., 2025).


1.2. Defining AI in School Management

Artificial intelligence, for this review, refers to computational systems
that perform tasks traditionally requiring human intelligence, including
pattern recognition, prediction, natural language understanding, and
decision support. The primary AI technologies identified in the
literature as relevant to school management include machine learning
(ML) algorithms for prediction and classification; predictive analytics
platforms for attendance, performance, and resource forecasting;
natural language processing (NLP) for communication, document
processing, and reporting; computer vision systems for attendance
automation and security monitoring; knowledge-based decision support
systems; and AI-driven business intelligence and analytics dashboards.
School management and organizational support processes encompasses
the full range of administrative, operational, governance, and leadership
activities that sustain the functioning of educational organizations. This
includes, but is not limited to, student information management; staff
scheduling and human resource administration; attendance monitoring
and reporting; budget planning and resource allocation; communication
and community engagement; decision support for principals and senior
leaders; school self-evaluation and performance monitoring;
compliance and regulatory reporting; and strategic planning.
Conceptualizations of AI in school management emerge most clearly
when examined through principals' concrete uses of data-driven
systems, automation tools, and generative applications in day-to-day
leadership. Studies on Principal Ethical AI Leadership (PEAIL)

describe AI as embedded in the same data infrastructures that underpin
teaching, assessment, and organizational dashboards, positioning AI
not as an isolated tool but as part of broader analytic platforms guiding
school-level decisions. Within this perspective, AI in school
management is defined operationally by the socio-technical
arrangements through which principals process information, allocate
resources, and exercise ethical agency in digitally mediated
environments (Ho, 2026). Empirical and advocacy-oriented work
portrays AI as supporting multiple managerial domains, AI-based
predictive analytics and risk-management tools contribute to evidence-
based decisions, particularly around forecasting and scenario analysis
for school improvement; operationally, automated systems for
scheduling, grading, and attendance exemplify AI applications that
increase efficiency and free leaders to concentrate on higher-level
strategic goals; in professional development, AI applications identify
teachers' learning needs and deliver personalized coaching through
virtual platforms, embedding staff development within continuous,
data-informed cycles; and in communication and equity, multilingual
messaging, stakeholder feedback collection, and student well-being
support via chatbots situate AI within relational and inclusion-oriented
dimensions of school management (Ho, 2026). PEAIL scholarship
refines this definition by emphasizing AI as both an object and a context
of ethical stewardship. PEAIL is described as a context-sensitive
practice that links micro-level moral agency with macro-level socio-
technical and policy frameworks, recognizing that opaque technologies,
structural inequities, and external governance pressures shape
principals' scope of action. Within this framing, AI in school
management encompasses tools whose outcomes affect instructional
and professional practices, organizational systems and governance, and
equity and inclusion. Responsible use of AI to support personalization,
differentiation, and student engagement is explicitly distinguished from
any replacement of pedagogical judgment, defining AI as augmentative
rather than substitutive in instructional decision processes.
Organizational and governance uses constitute another definitional
pillar. Ethical principals are described as implementing AI in
administrative functions such as scheduling, reporting, and resource

allocation while ensuring that efficiency gains do not erode
transparency or fairness. Acting as gatekeepers, they interpret AI-
generated data through a critical lens, uphold privacy and legal
compliance, and align AI deployment with school values and policy
frameworks. Under this view, AI in school management refers not only
to the technical systems themselves but also to the leadership practices
that filter, contextualize, and constrain their influence (Ho, 2026). A
complementary leadership framework elaborates AI-specific domains
aligned with professional standards for educational leaders. Within this
framework, AI is defined in terms of its role in data-informed decision-
making, operational efficiency, and administrative transformation.
Principals are expected to use AI tools to monitor key performance
indicators, automate routine tasks such as scheduling, attendance
reporting, and basic communications, and employ AI for compliance
tracking, budgeting, and resource allocation, always with human
judgment and ethical oversight (DeMatthews et al., 2026, p.3).
Conceptualizing AI in this way embeds it within strategic planning and
visioning, where principals cultivate a shared, people-centered vision
of AI integration, maintain a human-in-the-loop approach, and align AI
work with district and state policies. Other accounts describe AI as an
“extended brain” that helps principals process information, track
compliance and performance in real time, and make timely, data-
informed decisions, provided that findings are corroborated with human
stakeholders such as teachers and counselors. Generative AI systems
are additionally defined by their capacity to automate emails, meeting
summaries, budget tracking, and report writing, while requiring secure,
institutionally governed platforms for any interaction with sensitive
student data (DeMatthews et al., 2026, p.4). Across these strands, AI in
school management is thus delineated as a multi-domain ensemble of
analytic, automating, and generative tools whose meaning is co-
constituted by ethical leadership practices, governance structures, and
human-in-the-loop oversight.
1.3. Taxonomic Landscape: AI Applications in Educational
Leadership
Sposato (2025), in a comprehensive taxonomy of AI applications in
educational leadership, identifies multiple domains in which AI is

     Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review

shaping school leadership practice. Drawing on an extensive review of
literature, Sposato organizes AI applications along two axes: the level
of decision-making they support (operational, tactical, or strategic), and
the nature of the human–AI relationship (tool, assistant, collaborator, or
autonomous agent). This taxonomy provides a useful organizing frame
for the evidence synthesized in this review.
At the operational level, AI tools automate routine administrative tasks:
generating reports, monitoring attendance, processing scheduling
conflicts, managing communications, and tracking resource usage.
These tools function primarily as instruments that extend human
capacity without substantially altering the nature of decision-making.
At the tactical level, AI systems support more complex decisions:
analyzing student performance data, identifying at-risk students,
comparing resource allocation options, and evaluating staff
performance patterns. Here, the human–AI relationship begins to
involve genuine collaborative reasoning, with AI surfacing patterns and
options that school leaders evaluate and act upon. At the strategic level
— still largely aspirational in the current literature — AI systems might
support long-term school improvement planning, policy formation, and
organizational learning, potentially functioning as genuine decision
partners to school leaders.
1.4. The Human-AI Collaboration Imperative
Arar (2024) develops the concept of “symbiotic co-leadership,” arguing
that the most productive human–AI relationships in school contexts are
those in which each party contributes what it does best: AI contributes
computational power, pattern recognition, scalability, and consistency;
humans contribute contextual wisdom, ethical judgment, relational
intelligence, and creative adaptation. This framing is echoed across the
literature and constitutes the first major conceptual pillar of the field.
Dai & Rawolle (2025) argue that effective AI integration in school
management requires clarity about where human judgment is
irreplaceable, active co-design of AI tools with educators rather than
imposition of external vendor systems, and institutional structures that
ensure human oversight of AI-generated recommendations. This
framework is significant because it resists both techno-utopian accounts
of AI as a simple efficiency fix and dystopian accounts of AI as an

middle ground grounded in respect for the complexity of educational
leadership.


   2. Theoretical Foundation of School Management through AI
      perspective
   2.1. Educational Management Theories
Research on principal preparation for AI integration foregrounds the
Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the Unified Theory of
Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), and Diffusion of
Innovations (DOI) as core perspectives explaining how individual
perceptions, social influence, and systemic conditions shape technology
use in schools. These frameworks treat adoption as both technical and
socio-organizational, emphasizing perceived usefulness and ease of
use, organizational support, and the relative advantage and
compatibility of innovations within existing practices. Within
educational leadership scholarship, these adoption theories are
mobilized to argue that successful AI integration depends
simultaneously on personal attitudes, school culture, and structural
arrangements, making them foundational reference points for
management thinking in AI-rich environments.
Leadership-focused theories complement these adoption models by
specifying how principals can shape conditions for AI use. Instructional
leadership, transformational leadership, distributed leadership, and
social justice leadership are each invoked to conceptualize how school
leaders guide technology-related change. Instructional leadership
centers on improving teaching and learning, so AI tools are assessed in
terms of alignment with instructional goals and teacher growth.
Transformational leadership stresses vision and motivation; principals
who articulate ethical AI use and model innovation can help their
schools navigate adoption challenges. Distributed leadership
emphasizes teamwork and shared responsibility, positioning AI
initiatives as collective projects rather than individual decisions. Social
justice leadership introduces an explicit equity lens, orienting AI use

toward benefits for underserved students and protection against
exacerbated inequalities (DeMatthews et al., 2026, p.6).
Ethical leadership theories are reinterpreted in light of AI through the
conceptualization of Principal Ethical AI Leadership (PEAIL). PEAIL
is framed as socio-technical stewardship in which principals operate
within multi-level ecologies that link personal virtue, organizational
cultures, and external governance environments (Ho, 2026). Existing
work shows that ethical leadership in this context involves cultivating
trust, modeling principled behavior, and sustaining open dialogue, with
these relational qualities treated as preconditions for addressing AI-
related dilemmas around surveillance, automation, and equity.
Outcome-oriented syntheses group the effects of PEAIL into domains
such as instructional and professional practices, organizational systems
and governance, and equity and inclusion, illustrating how ethical
leadership norms translate into concrete decisions about
personalization, administrative automation, and the protection of
vulnerable students (Ho, 2026).
Methodologically oriented reviews of PEAIL expose important gaps in
how these theories are operationalized. Across 62 studies on AI and
ethical leadership, conceptual and qualitative designs dominate,
including nine conceptual pieces, seven commentaries, and 15–16
qualitative studies, with relatively few investigations directly
examining how school leaders make ethical decisions about AI tools.
Quantitative work largely addresses technology acceptance and
attitudes rather than ethical judgment, while theoretical engagement
spans 23 frameworks, of which Decision-Making Theory and
Transformational Leadership Theory appear only a handful of times
each. Critically oriented perspectives such as Critical Race Theory and
Intersectional Feminist Theory occur rarely, signaling that systemic
equity analyses remain marginal within current educational
management theorizing around AI (Ho, 2026). Synthesis across this
body of work yields a constrained but coherent picture of educational
management theories under digital disruption: technology adoption
models explain individual and organizational readiness for AI,
traditional leadership theories provide lenses on instructional,
transformational, distributed, and justice-oriented practices, and PEAIL

recasts ethical leadership as context-sensitive stewardship in AI-

        2.2. AI-Related Theoretical Frameworks
AI-related theorizing in education currently develops unevenly across
conceptual domains, with several strands explicitly identified as
underdeveloped or fragmented. A systematic synthesis of artificial
intelligence in education (AIEd) reviews reports that only 7.84% of
included work engages directly with AIEd theories, despite their critical
role in guiding the design, implementation, and evaluation of AI
systems for teaching and learning. This relative scarcity is linked to
difficulties in integrating abstract theoretical insights into real-world
educational settings and to a wider gap between technological
capabilities and educational theories that specify how, why, and under
what conditions AI can support learning processes effectively. Existing
AI-related frameworks in AIEd are often applied descriptively rather
than subjected to empirical testing: Cognitive Load Theory and Self-
Regulated Learning Theory are frequently invoked to justify the design
of AI-based tutoring and assessment systems, yet few studies assess
whether such interventions genuinely reduce cognitive load or foster
self-regulation in practice. Parallel fragmentation appears where Social
Agency Theory, TPACK, the Technology Acceptance Model, and
Constructivism are typically used in isolation, with limited attempts to
connect or integrate them into broader explanatory models of learning
with AI. This siloed application constrains comprehensive accounts of
how cognitive, emotional, and social factors jointly shape students'
engagement with AI tools (Huang et al., 2026, p.3).
Conceptual gaps also concern the temporal relationship between
technological innovation and theory-building. While AI techniques and
applications evolve rapidly, frameworks tailored specifically to AIEd
lag behind. Most studies continue to rely on established learning
theories rather than generating AI-informed conceptual models that

capture issues such as AI-enhanced feedback, human–AI co-agency, or
the dynamics of overreliance and critical engagement in AI-infused
environments. As a result, the field calls explicitly for theories extended
using evidence from learning analytics and human–AI collaboration,
with a view to explaining how AI can scaffold student thinking, support
teachers' metacognitive reflection, and sustain creativity and critical
thinking (Huang et al., 2026, p.12). Beyond individual learning
theories, wider framework architectures have been proposed to
structure AIEd research. One synthesis introduces a three-level
thematic framework distinguishing user-related themes from
developer-related themes at Level 1, then differentiating AI education
and literacy, AI applications, AI challenges, and AI technology
development at Level 2, and finally specifying more granular constructs
such as AI education courses, AI literacy, AI ethics, AI functions, AI
utilization processes, AI techniques, AI programming languages, and
AI operating platforms at Level 3. This hierarchical arrangement
clarifies how conceptual work on AI ethics, utilization processes, and
technical underpinnings can be systematically related rather than
treated as isolated topics, and it connects theoretical constructs
explicitly to functional categories such as prediction, detection,
classification, decision-making, and automated assessment (Huang et
al., 2026, p 5).
Methodological reflections on AI-related theoretical frameworks in
educational leadership exhibit a comparable pattern of conceptual
richness and empirical thinness. Reviews of PEAIL adopt established
ethical leadership theories but then ground the resulting conceptual
model in empirical patterns across 62 studies, rather than imposing
predefined assumptions (Ho, 2026). This approach yields a multi-
layered ecology in which individual dispositions, organizational
cultures, policy environments, and societal norms jointly shape how
principals address AI-mediated dilemmas, yet reviewers stress that
PEAIL remains a framework “informed by theory and guided by
evidence” rather than a theory rigorously validated through extensive
field data (Ho, 2026). Across these strands, research shows that AI-
related theoretical frameworks in education are characterized by
underdeveloped dedicated AIEd theories, descriptive rather than

    3. Research methodology
The research goal is to identify how AI is being implemented in school
management processes and to determine the extent to which it is
changing school management theory.
We used PRISMA framework. Searches were conducted in the
following databases: SpringerLink, Sage Journals, ScienceDirect
(Elsevier), ERIC (Education Resources Information Center), and
Google Scholar. The search was conducted using Boolean keyword
combinations including, but not limited to: “artificial intelligence”
AND “school management”; “AI” AND “educational leadership”;
“machine learning” AND “school administration”; “predictive
analytics” AND “educational decision-making”; “natural language
processing” AND “school organization”; “AI” AND “principal
leadership”; “automation” AND “school processes”; “AI ethics” AND
“education management”; “decision support systems” AND
“educational organizations”; and “AI” AND “administrative
efficiency” AND “schools.”
3.1.Eligibility Criteria
Inclusion criteria for the review were: (1) peer-reviewed articles or
book chapters published in English between January 2018 and April
2026; (2) empirical, theoretical, review, or framework articles
addressing the use of AI in school management, administrative
processes, educational leadership, or organizational support functions
in pre-university education settings; (3) articles providing sufficient
methodological detail and analytical substance to enable quality
assessment. Exclusion criteria were: (1) articles focused exclusively on

AI applications in classroom instruction or direct student learning
without addressing managerial or organizational dimensions; (2) grey
literature, conference proceedings, and non-peer-reviewed sources; (3)
duplicates; (4) articles in languages other than English.
3.2.Screening and Quality Assessment
Following the initial database search, approximately 400 records were
identified after deduplication. Title and abstract screening reduced this
to approximately 80 potentially eligible full-text articles. Full-text
review against the eligibility criteria resulted in a final corpus of
approximately 26 sources for qualitative synthesis. Studies were
assessed on criteria including clarity of research design, relevance of
evidence to the review question, transparency of methodology, and
appropriateness of conclusions relative to data. No studies were
excluded solely on quality grounds, but quality assessments informed
the weight given to different sources in the narrative synthesis.
Data Extraction and Synthesis
Data were extracted from each included source using a structured
extraction template covering: author(s), year, country/context, study
type, AI application described, educational management level
addressed, benefits reported, challenges identified, ethical dimensions
discussed, and key conclusions. Extracted data were analyzed
thematically, following the six-phase thematic analysis process
described by Braun and Clarke (2006): familiarization, generating
initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and
naming themes, and producing the report. Three overarching themes —
benefits, implementation challenges and equity implications, and the
human dimension of leadership — and multiple sub-themes were
identified, which structure the findings reported in Sections 5 through
8 of this article. Where the supplementary explainability, governance,
and organizational sources offered evidence that extended rather than
duplicated the core corpus — for example on financial and resource
management, student data management and cybersecurity,
interoperability, and the analysis of correlations between AI adoption
and school performance — these were integrated as additional sub-

3.3.Research Hypotheses for Future Empirical Investigation
We formulated three research hypotheses to guide future empirical inquiry.
These hypotheses are grounded in the patterns identified in the literature,
respond to the most significant gaps in current knowledge, and are designed
to be testable through rigorous empirical research designs. The convergent
evidence base assembled for this expanded review — spanning roughly forty
primary     and     secondary     sources     across    systematic   review,
explainability/ethics, and bibliometric/organizational management literatures
— reinforces rather than revises these hypotheses: the gains documented for
AI in school management are real but conditional, the human elements of
leadership remain foundational, and governance quality is decisive.

H1. AI Integration and Administrative Workload Reduction
The implementation of AI-driven automation and predictive analytics in
school management will significantly reduce administrative workload for
principals, thereby increasing the time and cognitive resources available for
instructional leadership, and thereby improving overall school effectiveness
as measured by teacher professional satisfaction, principal leadership quality
ratings, and student outcome indicators.
This hypothesis is grounded in the consistently documented finding that AI
tools reduce administrative burden in school management settings (Kafa,
2025, p.3; Machkour & Khdar, 2025, p.8; Reimers et al., 2025), combined
with the extensive literature on the relationship between principals'
instructional leadership time and school effectiveness. Future research should
test this hypothesis through longitudinal quasi-experimental designs
comparing schools that have implemented AI management tools with matched
comparison schools, measuring outcomes across multiple dimensions
including principal time allocation, leadership quality perceptions, and student
performance indicators.

H2. Governance Frameworks and Stakeholder Outcomes
Schools that adopt structured AI leadership frameworks — explicitly
addressing ethics, equity, community engagement, and operational
governance — will demonstrate higher stakeholder satisfaction, better


                                      253

      Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review
                                 Mariana Dogaru

decision-making quality, and more equitable student outcomes compared to
schools that adopt AI in management processes in an ad-hoc, unstructured
manner.
This hypothesis is grounded in the consistent emphasis in the literature on
governance, ethics, and structured implementation as determinants of AI
integration quality (DeMatthews et al., 2026, p.7; Dai & Rawolle, 2024,
p.1474; Arar, 2024, p.3). It implies that the quality of AI governance is as
important as the sophistication of the AI technology in determining outcomes
— a hypothesis that runs counter to the vendor-driven narrative that
technology quality is the primary determinant of AI integration success.
Future research should compare schools with structured AI governance
frameworks against those without, using mixed-methods designs that capture
both quantitative outcome indicators and qualitative stakeholder experience
data.

H3. Organizational Support Factors as Moderators
Organizational support factors — including staff AI literacy, adequate digital
infrastructure, clear ethical guidelines, and sustained professional
development — positively moderate the relationship between AI integration
in school management support processes and key performance indicators such
as resource optimization efficiency, principal decision quality, and teacher
collaboration, such that AI integration produces stronger positive outcomes in
schools with stronger organizational support conditions.
This hypothesis is grounded in multiple strands of evidence in the reviewed
literature (Kafa, 2025, p.4; Reimers et al., 2025; Wang, 2021, p.11; Arar,
2024, p.4) and reflects the theoretical insight that AI tools are not sufficient
conditions for improved outcomes: they are necessary but not sufficient, with
sufficiency contingent on organizational conditions that enable effective
human-AI collaboration. Future research should test this moderation
hypothesis through survey-based studies in large school samples, using
structural equation modeling or multilevel regression to estimate the
moderating effects of support conditions on the AI integration-outcomes
relationship.

Empirical work directly quantifying statistical correlations between AI
adoption and school performance remains scarce in current research on

educational leadership and AI. Reviews of Principal Ethical AI
Leadership (PEAIL) report that only a small subset of the 62 identified
studies examines how school leaders actually use AI tools in practice,
and fewer still trace these uses systematically to learning outcomes or
organizational performance indicators. Within this corpus, most
quantitative designs concentrate on technology acceptance or attitudes
rather than measurable effects on achievement, equity, or school
improvement metrics, which constrains any effort to compute or
generalize correlations between AI uptake and performance.
Available analyses instead describe AI-related outcomes qualitatively
across several levels. At the micro level, ethical leadership around AI
is reported to bolster teacher and student confidence in AI-mediated
processes, in part by reducing fears of bias and data misuse.
Organizationally, ethical deliberation is associated with stronger norms
of technology use and a more coherent moral-professional culture. At
policy and societal scales, ethical leadership linked to AI integration is
said to enhance legitimacy and accountability, positioning schools as
trustworthy actors within national digital education agendas. These
outcome descriptions, however, are seldom operationalized through
standardized performance indicators or statistical models that would
enable calculation of effect sizes or correlation coefficients.
Within substantive accounts of AI use in schools, PEAIL reviews
describe principals employing adaptive learning platforms, AI tutors,
generative tools, and predictive analytics for strategic planning, with
reported benefits including support for evidence-based decisions, risk
management, and automation of tasks such as scheduling, grading, and
attendance that free time for strategic goals. Separate work on AI-
enabled educational management highlights automated attendance,
course scheduling, and intelligent assessment systems that can provide
more comprehensive analyses of students' learning processes, habits,
and problem-solving than point-in-time examinations, potentially
informing long-term development (Feng & Li, 2024, p.50). Yet these
accounts primarily document functional opportunities and perceived
advantages; they do not provide paired quantitative measures of AI
adoption intensity and corresponding changes in test scores,

progression rates, or other outcome measures needed to establish
statistical correlations.
Overall, current evidence portrays a field in which AI adoption in
schools is expanding and is normatively linked to improved decision-
making, efficiency, and potentially more nuanced assessment, while
rigorous quantitative demonstrations of correlations with school
performance are largely absent. Reviews consistently recommend
longitudinal, comparative, and mixed-methods research incorporating
systematic indicators, equity measures, stakeholder trust metrics, and
learning outcomes, so that relationships between AI integration and
school performance can be assessed empirically rather than inferred.

4.2.Benefits of AI in school management
4.2.1Administrative Efficiency and Workload Reduction


The most consistently documented benefit of AI in school management
across the literature is improved administrative efficiency. Studies from
multiple national contexts report that AI-powered tools can
significantly reduce the time school leaders and administrative staff
spend on routine, repetitive, and low-complexity tasks, thereby freeing
cognitive and temporal resources for higher-order leadership activities.
Machkour & Khdar (2025) synthesize evidence from multiple empirical
studies to conclude that AI-driven administrative tools reduce average
time spent on routine tasks by substantial margins. The authors cite
evidence of automated report generation, intelligent scheduling
systems, and AI-powered communication platforms as examples of
tools that streamline management workflows. Critically, the authors
note that these efficiency gains are not automatic: they depend on
adequate system design, staff training, and integration with existing
administrative infrastructures.
Reimers and colleagues (2025) present case evidence from diverse
national contexts across Latin America, Africa, and Southeast Asia,
documenting how schools in under-resourced settings have used AI
tools to manage enrollment data, generate compliance reports, and
automate communication with parents. Notably, the authors identify
that administrative efficiency gains are particularly significant in

     Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review
                                Mariana Dogaru

schools where administrative staffing is thin and principals are directly
responsible for tasks that, in wealthier contexts, are handled by
dedicated administrative teams. This finding complicates simplistic
narratives of AI as primarily a luxury of well-resourced contexts and
suggests that AI tools, if designed and deployed thoughtfully, could
have substantial equity implications.
Kafa (2025) surveyed school leaders about their perceptions and
experiences of using an AI tool for management tasks. The findings
reveal that a majority of participating principals perceived the AI tool
as useful for reducing routine administrative burden, with particular
appreciation expressed for features that automated scheduling,
generated standardized reports, and supported attendance monitoring.
Importantly, Kafa (2025) notes that perceptions of usefulness were
significantly moderated by principals' AI literacy and their confidence
in interpreting AI-generated outputs, underscoring the dependency of
efficiency gains on human capacity to engage with AI productively.
A complementary leadership framework specifies how this efficiency
potential should be operationalized. Guidance for principals describes
domain-level expectations that AI should streamline communications,
scheduling, compliance tracking, budgeting, resource allocation, and
attendance reporting so that staff can redirect time toward instruction
and relationship building. Within this framing, automation is not
positioned as an end in itself but as a means to protect staff work time
and align operations with school improvement goals and district
priorities, with explicit attention to data accuracy, fairness, and legal
compliance (DeMatthews et al., 2026, p.8). One set of practices focuses
on data-informed decision-making supported by automated dashboards
and monitoring tools: principals are advised to use AI systems to track
key performance indicators in strategic plans and to identify trends in
areas such as attendance or discipline, while always applying human
judgment to interpret patterns and confirm risk flags with teachers and
counselors before intervening. AI is further characterized as an
“extended brain” that assists school leaders in processing large volumes
of information and making timely decisions, provided that findings are
corroborated through stakeholder feedback and strong oversight
(DeMatthews et al., 2026, p.8). These recommendations underscore a

human-in-the-loop paradigm in which automated analytics remain
subordinate to professional judgment and community values.
Another cluster of contributions elaborates concrete administrative
tasks amenable to automation. Suggested uses include AI-generated
basic emails, meeting summaries, budget tracking, and the rapid
creation of master schedules that consider teacher availability, student
needs, and compliance requirements. Generative systems are also
presented as tools for preparing reports, analyzing student data, setting
up professional development schedules, and tracking regulatory
compliance (DeMatthews et al., 2026, p.10). At a broader operational
level, school leaders are encouraged to automate routine scheduling,
attendance reporting, and basic communications, and to embed AI into
transactional functions linked to resource stewardship and operations
management, explicitly tied to professional standards that call for
actionable data systems, strategic staffing, and technology use to
improve operations while maintaining privacy, security, and legal
conformity (DeMatthews et al., 2026, p.11).
Evidence from higher-level analyses of education systems provides a
complementary view. It describes automated management as a major
opportunity domain, alongside personalized learning, global resource
sharing, and teacher professional development support. In this account,
AI systems perform time-consuming tasks such as course scheduling,
student attendance, and grade evaluation, using technologies including
facial recognition for attendance and natural language processing for
automatic grading; these tools are reported to reduce teachers'
administrative workload and enable greater focus on instruction and
individualized tutoring. Intelligent assessment systems are said to move
beyond point-in-time examinations by continuously analyzing students'
learning processes, habits, and problem-solving patterns to deliver more
comprehensive and accurate evaluations that can inform long-term
development (Feng & Li, 2024, p.49). To structure empirical work on
automation impacts, general methodological guidance such as the PICO
framework — specifying population, intervention, comparator, and
outcome as core components of focused questions — can be adapted to
support rigorous evaluation of AI-based administrative interventions
relative to traditional processes (Parambil et al., 2024, p.5). Beyond

4.2.2.Data-Driven Decision Support
A second major domain of documented AI benefit in school
management concerns decision support. AI systems — particularly
those incorporating machine learning, predictive analytics, and
business intelligence dashboards — can process and synthesize large
volumes of educational data to provide school leaders with timely,
evidence-grounded insights that would be difficult or impossible to
generate manually.
Wang (2021) examines the intersection of AI and data-informed
decision-making in educational leadership. Drawing on a detailed case
analysis, Wang identifies several ways in which AI systems can
enhance the quality of decisions by school leaders: by surfacing non-
obvious patterns in student performance data, by enabling rapid
comparison of outcomes across demographic subgroups, and by
generating probabilistic forecasts of future trends. Crucially, however,
Wang's analysis is framed as a “cautionary tale”: the author documents
how an overconfident reliance on AI-generated data can distort
decision-making, particularly when leaders lack the statistical literacy
to interrogate AI outputs critically or when the underlying data contain
systematic biases. Wang's article makes a powerful case that AI
enhances decision quality only when it is combined with human
analytical skill, contextual knowledge, and healthy epistemic
skepticism.
DeMatthews et al (2026) present a six-domain framework for AI
integration in school leadership that explicitly includes “data-informed

decision-making” as one of its core domains. The authors argue that AI
can support more equitable decision-making by making patterns of
differential treatment, resource distribution, and outcome disparities
visible to leaders who might otherwise be unaware of them. They note,
however, that this potential is contingent on data quality,
representational equity in training datasets, and the interpretive capacity
of school leaders.
4.2.3.Process optimization in school
Beyond efficiency and decision support, the literature identifies a third
category of AI benefit, the optimization of specific operational
processes within school management. These include student attendance
monitoring, staff scheduling, resource allocation, performance
monitoring, and community communication.
Computer vision and machine learning-based attendance systems —
using face recognition, deep learning algorithms, and AI cameras —
have been documented in multiple studies as enabling more accurate,
efficient, and less administratively burdensome monitoring of student
attendance. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been applied
to automate classroom attendance marking, reducing both the
administrative load on teachers and the error rates associated with
manual attendance systems. Predictive models have been used to
forecast attendance patterns, enabling proactive intervention for
students at risk of chronic absenteeism.
In the area of scheduling, AI systems have been used to generate
optimal timetables that minimize conflicts, maximize resource
utilization, and accommodate complex constraints across large schools.
In resource allocation, machine learning-based forecasting models have
supported more accurate budgeting and planning by predicting
enrollment trends, maintenance needs, and staffing requirements. In
communication, natural language processing tools have enabled
automated generation of parent newsletters, personalized progress
communications, and multilingual translation of school documents,
reducing the administrative burden associated with community
engagement while potentially improving the quality and accessibility of
communication.

4.2.4.Financial and resource management
Financial and resource management in AI-enabled school
administration aligns closely with broader patterns of AI-supported
decision-making and operational efficiency observed across sectors.
Guidance for principals positions AI as a means to assist with
budgeting, compliance tracking, and resource allocation, while
retaining human oversight and clear alignment with school
improvement goals and district priorities (DeMatthews et al., 2026,
p.7). Within this framing, automating transactional tasks is intended to
protect staff time for instruction and relationships, not to replace
professional judgment in financial decisions.
Evidence from manufacturing and logistics illustrates how AI augments
financial stewardship by integrating large data streams for cost control
and risk reduction. In Industry 4.0 environments, AI systems integrate
data from information technology, smart sensors, and interconnected
devices to support operational efficiency, supply chain coordination,
and adaptation to changing environments. Automation of warehouse
robotics, visual inspection, fault detection, and predictive maintenance
reduces costs associated with unanticipated interruptions and
operational errors, with financial benefits arising from improved
reliability and lower disruption-related expenses. Within supply chain
management, AI-based analysis of historical and real-time data
supports forecasting, resource planning, and optimization of
coordination, yielding more efficient allocations of inventory, transport,
and production capacity, and generating cost savings and greater
responsiveness to market fluctuations. Generative AI expands this
financial and resource lens by enabling scenario analysis around
political, social, or economic changes: such systems can model possible
disruptions in demand or supplier availability, supporting risk
assessment and the design of more efficient distribution strategies and
logistics routes in light of external factors such as weather patterns and
environmental constraints, as well as internal needs including cost
reduction and management of peak demand. These applications show
how AI-enabled scenario planning can inform budgetary contingencies
and investment decisions linked to infrastructure, transportation, and

inventory buffers in complex organizational settings (Aguado-García et
al., 2026, p.27-32).
Comparative organizational analyses in management information
systems further underline how AI capabilities relate to financial
performance. Classifications of AI in management highlight decision
support systems, analytical and statistical models, optimization
techniques, and cost–benefit analysis as central elements of “production
optimization and analytics” and “business economics and transition to
economy 4.0,” where decision-making, value creation, cost
engineering, and budget control are key cluster components. Within
these clusters, analytical models, stochastic systems, and uncertainty
analysis contribute to multi-criteria evaluations of profitability, risk,
and investment strategies (Padovano & Cardamone, 2024, p.10). Such
structures parallel school-level needs to evaluate AI investments, weigh
operational savings against licensing and training costs, and monitor
financial impacts over time.
Human resource management cases focused on compensation,
performance management, and benefits provide additional insight into
AI-supported financial stewardship in people-intensive organizations.
Scenario-based analyses of compensation management and benefits
management stress that pay structures, incentives, and benefits
enhancements must adhere to industry standards, best practices, and
regulatory constraints while allocating resources efficiently and
following transparent implementation timelines. Performance
management and professional development applications similarly
emphasize that system implementation requires careful resource
allocation for training managers and employees, and must align with
core organizational objectives such as precision, innovation, and
strategic capacity (Aguinis et al., 2024, p. 5). These HR-oriented
examples underscore that AI-informed financial and resource decisions
need to integrate contextual factors, regulatory compliance, and long-
term talent development goals rather than focusing solely on short-term
cost reductions.

4.2.5.Student Data Management as an application domain
Student data management constitutes a fourth application domain in
which AI offers school leaders genuine operational benefits, while
simultaneously introducing the privacy, security, and ethical risks
examined. AI systems support administrative integration of large,
sensitive data streams: managing enrollment records, tracking
compliance with attendance and reporting obligations, and feeding
analytics dashboards that inform leadership decisions. Within e-
learning and institutional platforms, technical interventions target the
secure management of students' digital traces, including improving the
accuracy and security of attendance systems by combining face
recognition with location detection on mobile devices, developing
anonymous authentication schemes that validate learners while
minimizing latency, and integrating multi-modal biometric techniques
into learning management systems to maintain identity assurance across
sessions. Additional work develops privacy-preserving algorithms for
learning analytics datasets in order to balance student privacy protection
with the analytical usefulness of the resulting information (Parambil et
al., 2024, p.3). Complementary efforts use blockchain to manage
educational records and digital diplomas and to enhance transparency
around how learning analytics data are treated, for example through
visual icons that communicate data practices to stakeholders (Parambil
et al., 2024, p.4).

From a management perspective, the operational value of these tools —
more accurate attendance records, more secure and efficient handling
of enrollment and assessment data, and richer analytics for leadership
decision-making — is real, but it is inseparable from the governance,
consent, and security obligations discussed later in this review. As such,
student data management functions simultaneously as a benefit domain
and as one of the most consequential sites of ethical risk in AI-enabled
school management.
4.2.6.School Leadership support and strategic capacity
The literature also documents emerging evidence that AI can support
the strategic and leadership dimensions of school management, not just
the operational and administrative. AI-powered analytics dashboards
can provide principals with real-time visibility into school performance
indicators — attendance rates, grade distributions, disciplinary
incidents, staff wellbeing signals — that support more responsive and
evidence-informed leadership.
DeMatthews et al (2026) articulate a vision in which AI serves as a
“strategic partner” for school leaders, augmenting their capacity to
identify improvement priorities, monitor implementation fidelity, and
evaluate the impact of leadership decisions. The authors acknowledge
that this vision is largely aspirational given the current state of AI
development and deployment in schools, but they argue that it
represents a legitimate and important direction for both research and
practice. Similarly, Sposato (2025) envisions AI systems that can
support “adaptive strategic planning” in schools — systems that learn
from historical decision patterns, model the likely consequences of
different strategic choices, and provide leaders with scenario-based
decision support.

4.2.7.Implementation Challenges, Ethical Concerns, and Equity
Implications
a. Data quality, privacy, and security
Across the literature, concerns about data quality, privacy, and security
emerge as among the most consistently identified challenges to AI
integration in school management. AI systems are only as good as the
data they are trained on and operate with; in many school contexts, data
are incomplete, inconsistently recorded, siloed across incompatible
systems, or reflective of historical biases. Data quality limitations can
undermine the reliability of AI-generated predictions and
recommendations, and in some cases may generate systematically
misleading outputs.




                                        266

     Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review
                                Mariana Dogaru

Privacy concerns are particularly acute in school settings, where data
subjects are often minors, where data contain sensitive information
about student learning needs, behavioral histories, family
circumstances, and health status, and where the consequences of data
misuse can be severe. Regulatory frameworks such as FERPA in the
United States, GDPR in the European Union, and analogous legislation
in other jurisdictions impose significant requirements on how student
data may be collected, processed, stored, and shared. AI systems that
process student data must comply with these frameworks, and the
compliance burden is non-trivial, particularly for smaller schools and
districts with limited legal and technical expertise.
Wang (2021) documents cases in which school leaders were presented
with AI-generated analytics that obscured, rather than revealed, the
limitations and uncertainties in underlying data, leading to
overconfident decision-making. This finding underscores the
importance of what Wang calls “data literacy” and “AI literacy” for
school leaders: the capacity to understand not just what AI systems
output, but how those outputs are generated, what assumptions they
embed, and where they may be unreliable.
Principal Ethical AI Leadership (PEAIL) scholarship positions school
leaders as socio-technical stewards whose responsibilities include
anticipating and mitigating risks related to data privacy, algorithmic
bias, and informed consent, as well as setting clear policies and ensuring
compliance with legal and social standards. Within this framing, data
protection is integral to safeguarding vulnerable students and
reinforcing justice in AI-enabled schooling, not a purely legal or
technical add-on (Ho, 2026). Advocacy-oriented analyses of PEAIL
specify data privacy and security as a core domain of ethical action:
authors argue that principals must establish robust data protection
protocols, including anonymization and consent management, in order
to meet legal and ethical obligations for both students and staff.
Commentators caution against surveillance-based AI systems and call
for greater transparency regarding algorithmic design and data
sourcing, while also insisting that all AI tools be subject to ongoing
ethical scrutiny and compliance checks under principal oversight (Ho,
2026). Further practice-oriented recommendations propose



                                   267

      Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review
                                 Mariana Dogaru

institutionalizing governance routines such as data-protection
protocols, data-minimization workflows, algorithmic impact
assessments, audit logs, and regular “ethics rounds” that review high-
stakes uses of AI, together with explicit codification of non-delegable
decisions and documented professional judgments when AI outputs are
overridden (Ho, 2026).
Evidence indicates that principals, teachers, and students encounter
persistent challenges in data ethics and security, particularly around the
handling of sensitive student information collected by AI systems. Such
data remains vulnerable to misuse, and schools frequently lack robust
mechanisms to ensure informed consent when deploying AI tools.
Advocacy-oriented work contends that inadequate oversight and
opaque AI processes expose school leaders to significant ethical risk
during technology adoption, especially where monitoring practices like
facial recognition and intensive analytics become normalized and
threaten the ethical integrity of school environments (Ho, 2026).
Cybersecurity analyses focused on educational settings catalogue a
broad spectrum of technical threats that directly affect student data
management. Documented risks include data breaches, unauthorized
access to biometric and location data, hacking, phishing, malware,
denial-of-service and distributed denial-of-service attacks, information
gathering, cross-site scripting, SQL injection, and malicious PDF
distribution. Table-based summaries further identify privacy threats in
cloud storage and data sharing, identity theft, fraud, and unauthorized
access in online learning, as well as unauthorized screen recording of
course videos and exam fraud via impersonation (Parambil et al., 2024,
p.4).
Studies call for robust security architectures that balance efficiency with
privacy through encrypted storage, secure authentication, and scalable
network designs, while also warning that explainable deep neural
network approaches for threat detection can impose additional
computational and infrastructural burdens. Ethical use of biometric data
in student authentication receives particular attention, with authors
stressing the need for explicit consent, data usage transparency, and
long-term security evaluation of blockchain or biometric systems to
avoid inadvertent exposure or misuse (Parambil et al., 2024, p.8).

Synthesis of cybersecurity recommendations for managers of
educational systems and designers of AI-driven protection tools
underscores several actionable priorities: comprehensive training
programs for educators, students, and staff should ensure effective and
informed use of AI-based cybersecurity tools; robust ethical guidelines
must respect student privacy, comply with legal frameworks, and
provide transparent communication about AI uses in security;
interdisciplinary collaboration between AI experts, cybersecurity
professionals, and educators is advised to develop tailored solutions for
education, with machine learning algorithms such as random forests
and deep neural networks employed to improve real-time threat

models for distance learning emphasize coordinated assurance

analyses of AI-related ethical challenges in schools emphasize that
principals often lack clear frameworks to govern data-intensive AI

necessarily align with educational or ethical values. This dependence,
combined with opaque AI decision processes, complicates
accountability when harmful or biased outcomes emerge from student
data processing (Ho, 2026, p.14; Parambil et al., 2024, p.7). From a
management perspective, student data handling is thus not only a
technical cybersecurity problem but also a domain where ethical
leadership, informed consent, transparency, and equitable governance
must be integrated with advanced authentication, encryption, analytics,

b.Algorithmic bias, discrimination, and accountability
A second cluster of challenges concerns the potential for AI systems
used in school management to embed, perpetuate, or amplify
discriminatory patterns. Algorithmic bias — the systematic tendency of
AI systems to produce outputs that disadvantage particular groups —
can arise from biased training data, from design choices that embed
historically unequal patterns as normative, from the selection of
variables that serve as proxies for protected characteristics, or from the
interaction of multiple individually defensible design choices that
combine to produce discriminatory effects.
In school management contexts, the risk of algorithmic bias is
particularly salient in three areas: student classification and tracking
systems that use AI to assign students to programs or interventions; staff
evaluation and performance management systems that use AI-
generated metrics to assess teacher effectiveness; and predictive
systems that identify at-risk students for targeted support. In each of
these areas, if the AI system is trained on historical data that reflects
discriminatory patterns — for example, if students from particular
racial, ethnic, or socioeconomic backgrounds were historically over-
represented in disciplinary data — the system may reproduce and
legitimize those patterns through ostensibly objective algorithmic
outputs.
DeMatthews et al (2026) devote substantial attention to what they call
“ethical and equitable AI leadership,” arguing that school leaders have
a professional and moral responsibility to critically interrogate the
fairness implications of any AI tool they adopt or permit to be used in
their schools. The authors advocate for regular algorithmic audits,
diverse and representative data governance processes, transparency
requirements for AI vendors, and clear lines of human accountability
for decisions made with AI assistance. They propose a six-domain
framework — encompassing ethics, equity, operations, curriculum,
community, and leadership development — that positions ethical
accountability not as a constraint on AI adoption but as its essential
foundation.

Analyses of AI in online and higher education security contexts

Authors discussed within this literature describe how AI systems can
reflect or amplify existing societal biases, thereby generating unfair
outcomes when deployed in educational settings; these findings frame
bias not as an abstract possibility but as a concrete mechanism through
which algorithmic decision-making can undermine equity. Concerns
about fairness arise alongside related demands for transparency and
accountability: institutional responsibility in the deployment of AI
technologies requires clearly articulated policies that specify how
systems will be used and what impacts they may have on student
autonomy and academic integrity, alongside transparency in AI
decision-making processes and human oversight to correct errors or
biases, so that students, parents, and educators can understand AI-
driven outcomes and have channels to question or challenge them
(Parambil et al., 2024, p.9). In this view, fairness is inseparable from
visibility into algorithmic operations and from accountable human
governance structures.
PEAIL scholarship translates these fairness issues into leadership
practice. Within the equity and inclusion outcome domain, PEAIL is
described as ensuring that AI tools are used to promote equity rather
than reinforce exclusion. When guided ethically, AI can improve access
for multilingual learners, support diverse learning needs, and foster
inclusive communication, but leaders remain responsible for
anticipating and mitigating risks related to data privacy, algorithmic
bias, and informed consent. Ethical leadership involves setting clear
policies, raising awareness, and ensuring compliance with legal and
social norms, with explicit attention to protecting vulnerable students
and reinforcing justice in AI-enabled schooling (Ho, 2026). Broader
management analyses of AI reinforce the idea that biased training data
can drive discriminatory outcomes: systems can perpetuate pre-existing
biases in their training datasets, for instance in hiring contexts where
gender or racial prejudices embedded in historical data lead to
discriminatory recommendations. Automation bias is highlighted as a
particular danger — decision-makers may over-trust AI outputs

algorithmic recommendations without adequate scrutiny. This

need for interpretability and clear lines of responsibility (Islami &

stewardship operates in multi-level ecologies. PEAIL is situated within

Within this setting, ethical leadership is reconceptualized as socio-
technical stewardship that connects micro-level moral agency with
macro-level policy and infrastructural conditions. Outcome syntheses
show that principals who act as gatekeepers interpret AI-generated data
critically, align AI use with school values and policy frameworks, and
resist blind reliance on algorithms in decisions about funding, staffing,
and resource allocation. Under these conditions, AI-supported
efficiencies are prevented from compromising transparency or fairness,
and equity goals such as inclusion and justice are advanced rather than
undermined (Ho, 2026). Evidence across these strands indicates that
addressing bias and fairness in AI requires not only technical mitigation
of discriminatory patterns but also robust ethical leadership,
institutional accountability, and policy mechanisms that foreground
equity in AI design and use.
c.Resistance, trust, and professional culture
The literature identifies a third set of implementation challenges related
to the human and cultural dimensions of AI adoption in schools.
Teacher and administrator resistance to AI tools is documented in
multiple studies, arising from concerns about job displacement,
surveillance, deskilling, loss of professional autonomy, and distrust of
opaque algorithmic systems. This resistance is not irrational: it reflects
legitimate professional concerns about the implications of AI adoption
for the nature of educational work.
Arar (2024) argues that resistance to AI in school management is often
a symptom of deeper organizational dynamics — cultures of distrust,
inadequate change management, insufficient consultation with staff,

and a perceived imposition of technological change from above. Arar's
human-machine symbiosis framework emphasizes that successful AI
integration requires not just technical implementation but cultural and
relational work: building shared understandings of the purposes and
limitations of AI, creating space for critical dialogue about AI adoption,
and ensuring that staff feel that their expertise and professional
judgment are valued rather than threatened.
Kafa (2025) reports that among the school leaders surveyed in Cyprus,
willingness to use AI tools was significantly associated with prior
positive experience with technology, institutional support structures,
and confidence in the tool's reliability. These findings align with the
broader technology adoption literature in suggesting that perceived
usefulness, perceived ease of use, and trust are key determinants of AI
tool adoption, and that all three can be enhanced through appropriate
training, support, and communication.
Infrastructure, digital divide, and contextual Equity
A fourth domain of implementation challenges concerns the
infrastructural and contextual conditions required for effective AI
deployment in school management. AI tools require reliable digital
infrastructure — broadband connectivity, up-to-date hardware,
interoperable software systems, and technical support — that is
unevenly distributed across and within educational systems globally.
Schools in under-resourced communities, rural settings, and lower-
income countries often lack the basic technological prerequisites for AI
adoption, raising serious concerns about whether AI may deepen rather
than mitigate existing educational inequities.
Reimers and colleagues (2025) document this challenge explicitly in
their analysis of AI and school management in the Global South. While
acknowledging the potential for AI to address specific management
challenges in under-resourced settings, the authors note that realizing
this potential requires substantial prior investment in digital
infrastructure, technical capacity, and supportive policy frameworks.
Without these investments, AI adoption risks benefiting primarily the
most advantaged schools and systems, widening digital and resource
divides.

The equity implications of AI adoption in school management extend

language needs of schools in other contexts. The absence of diverse
voices in AI design and governance is a systemic problem that requires
deliberate corrective action through inclusive design processes,
community consultation, and diversification of the AI development
pipeline.
Policy gaps and governance deficits
The literature consistently identifies the absence of adequate policy
frameworks for AI in school management as a significant systemic
challenge. Despite the rapid pace of AI adoption in education, many
school systems lack explicit policies addressing data governance,
algorithmic accountability, AI procurement standards, staff AI literacy
requirements, and ethical frameworks for AI use. This policy vacuum
creates risks: schools may adopt AI tools without adequate due
diligence, vendor interests may shape AI adoption decisions in the
absence of public regulatory frameworks, and accountability for AI-
related harms may be diffuse and unenforceable.
Multiple authors call for the development of robust policy frameworks
at national and district levels that set clear standards for AI adoption in
schools, require transparency from AI vendors, protect student and staff
data, establish accountability mechanisms, and ensure equitable access.
DeMatthews et al (2026) propose that these policy frameworks be co-
developed with school leaders, teachers, students, families, and
community members rather than designed exclusively by technologists
and bureaucrats, emphasizing the importance of democratic
participation in AI governance.

5.The Human Dimension: Leadership, Ethics, and Human-AI
Symbiosis
5.1. What AI Cannot Do in Educational Leadership
One of the most intellectually significant contributions of the recent
literature on AI in school management is its sustained attention to the
boundaries of AI capability in educational leadership contexts. While
acknowledging the efficiency and decision-support benefits
documented in the preceding sections, multiple scholars emphasize
with considerable force that there are dimensions of school leadership
that AI cannot replicate, and that protecting and cultivating these
dimensions must be a central priority of any responsible AI integration
strategy. Arar (2024) argues that the “human” in educational leadership
encompasses at least four irreducible capacities: moral imagination —
the ability to envision what is good and just in complex situations
without algorithmic guidance; relational intelligence — the capacity to
build trust, navigate conflict, and sustain authentic professional
relationships with students, teachers, families, and communities;
contextual wisdom — deep familiarity with the particular history,
culture, and community that gives meaning to events and decisions in a


                                        280

      Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review
                                 Mariana Dogaru

specific school; and creative adaptation — the ability to respond
inventively to unprecedented situations for which no historical pattern
exists in training data. None of these capacities is reducible to pattern
recognition, prediction, or optimization, and all are essential to effective
school leadership.
Wang (2021) makes a complementary argument through the lens of
epistemological humility, contending that school leaders who become
overly dependent on AI-generated analytics risk losing the capacity for
the kind of situated, holistic, and relational knowing that constitutes
genuine expertise in educational leadership. When a principal's
decision-making becomes dominated by what the dashboard says, the
rich texture of professional judgment — informed by direct
observation, relational knowledge, historical memory, and ethical
commitment — may be gradually eroded. Wang's cautionary tale is not
an argument against AI in schools, but a powerful case for approaching
AI integration with deliberate attention to the preservation of
professional wisdom.
5.2. Frameworks for Ethical AI Leadership in Schools
In response to both the genuine potential and the genuine risks of AI in
school management, several scholars have proposed frameworks for
what they variously call “ethical AI leadership,” “responsible AI
integration,” or “human-centered AI governance” in educational
organizations. These frameworks converge on a set of core principles
that can guide school leaders, district administrators, and policymakers
in navigating AI adoption responsibly.
DeMatthews et al (2026) propose the most comprehensive framework
to date in the educational leadership literature. Their six-domain
framework addresses: (1) Ethics — commitment to transparency,
accountability, and the protection of human dignity in AI decision-
making; (2) Equity — active interrogation of whether AI tools advance
or undermine equitable outcomes for all students; (3) Operations —
thoughtful integration of AI into administrative and management
workflows; (4) Curriculum — attention to the implications of AI for
what and how students learn; (5) Community — inclusive engagement
with families and communities in AI governance processes; and (6)
Leadership Development — building school leaders' capacity to


                                    281

     Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review
                                Mariana Dogaru

navigate AI-integrated environments with wisdom, courage, and ethical
commitment. The framework is notable for its insistence that ethics and
equity are not add-ons to AI governance but its foundational pillars.
Dai & Rawolle (2024) contribute a complementary framework focused
specifically on the human-AI relationship in administrative decision-
making. They propose a four-quadrant model distinguishing between
types of decisions on the basis of complexity and stakes, arguing that
the appropriate role of AI varies systematically across these quadrants.
For routine, low-stakes decisions, AI automation is appropriate and
beneficial; for complex, high-stakes decisions — particularly those
involving individual students, staff members, or community
relationships — human judgment must remain primary, with AI serving
at most an advisory role. The framework offers practical guidance for
school leaders navigating the question of which decisions to delegate to
AI and which to preserve as fundamentally human responsibilities.
A further contribution to this body of frameworks comes from work on
Principal Ethical AI Leadership (PEAIL), which repositions school
principals from heroic individual moral managers toward stewards of
socio-technical systems (Ho, 2026). On this view, ethical AI leadership
is operationalized not only through individual values but through
concrete governance routines: institutionalized algorithmic audits,
recurring “ethics rounds” in which AI-supported decisions affecting
students or staff are collectively reviewed, and explicit registers of
“non-delegable decisions” — categories of decision that must always
retain a documented human-in-the-loop checkpoint regardless of AI
recommendation confidence. Complementary formal tools, such as the
Isabelle Infrastructure Framework (Kammüller & Satija, 2023, p.9),
offer methods for representing data flows, actors, locations, and
otherwise opaque algorithmic functions as inspectable state structures
amenable to counterfactual analysis, thereby giving ethical AI
leadership frameworks a concrete technical complement: governance
principles are only as effective as the inspectability of the systems they
govern.
5.3. AI Literacy as a Leadership Competency
A theme that emerges with increasing insistence across the literature is
the importance of AI literacy as a core competency for contemporary


                                   282

     Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review
                                Mariana Dogaru

school leaders. AI literacy in this context does not refer to technical
programming ability, but to the conceptual, critical, and practical
knowledge that enables leaders to understand what AI systems do, how
they generate their outputs, what assumptions and limitations they
embody, how to evaluate their outputs critically, and how to make
ethically informed decisions about AI adoption and governance.
Sposato (2025) argues that AI literacy should be treated as a
professional development priority for educational leaders, analogous to
the emphasis on data literacy that became central to educational
leadership preparation in the early twenty-first century. Just as school
leaders are expected to be able to read and critically interpret student
performance data, they should be expected to be able to read and
critically interpret AI-generated analytics. This requires investment in
both pre-service and in-service professional learning programs that
build leaders' AI literacy in educationally relevant and practically
grounded ways.
Kafa's (2025) empirical findings from Cyprus suggest that AI literacy
is not merely a nice-to-have but a determinant of whether AI tools
actually deliver their promised benefits. Principals who lacked
confidence in understanding and interpreting AI outputs tended either
to over-rely on AI recommendations without critical evaluation or to
reject AI tools entirely. Both responses are suboptimal and both reflect
the inadequacy of professional preparation for AI-integrated leadership,
with clear implications for educational leadership preparation
programs, professional development providers, and school systems
designing AI integration strategies.
5.4.Human-AI Collaboration, Explainability, and the Symbiosis
Phase
Human-AI collaboration in educational leadership can be framed not as
a simple extension of earlier, purely human-centered phases of ethical
leadership, but as a qualitative reconfiguration of ethical practice in
which algorithmic systems actively participate in assessment,
prediction, and decision-making. Ho (2026) describes this
configuration as a “Human-AI Symbiosis and Ethical Governance
Phase” that redistributes agency between human principals and non-
human systems within hybrid socio-technical environments. AI in


                                   283

     Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review
                                Mariana Dogaru

school management in this phase comprises discriminative models such
as predictive analytics for attendance, behavior, and achievement;
adaptive learning platforms and intelligent tutoring systems that
automate feedback; and generative models such as large-language
systems producing text, images, or code. Each of these architectures
exhibits distinct profiles of transparency, traceability, and
accountability, so collaborative governance requires differentiated
forms of oversight rather than a single uniform policy.
Within this framing, PEAIL operates as a cross-cutting lens connecting
personal virtue and professional judgment with systemic accountability,
cultural pluralism, and technological mediation. Collaboration with AI
is not merely tool use but an ongoing negotiation in which leaders
interpret, negotiate, and at times resist pressures created by digital
platforms, vendor infrastructures, and regulatory structures. The
influencing factors span individual, organizational, policy, and socio-
cultural levels, and are bounded by an overarching governance
environment comprising market dominance, algorithmic opacity, cross-
jurisdictional regulation, and global normative commitments to
fairness, inclusion, and human dignity — boundary conditions that
shape the possibilities and constraints of human-AI collaboration in
everyday leadership.
Within this ecology, principals encounter recurring ethical challenges
precisely at the points where humans and AI systems intersect: data
privacy and surveillance, algorithmic bias, unequal access, professional
autonomy, and transparency all arise as dilemmas in which algorithmic
outputs and school-level values must be jointly navigated. Strategic
responses cast human-AI collaboration as mediated through explicit
leadership practices — strengthening human oversight, protecting data,
fostering inclusion, sustaining human-centered school cultures,
ensuring transparent procedures, building AI literacy and ethical
awareness, and working to bridge digital divides, all refracted through
ongoing negotiations with vendors and compliance regimes. When such
strategies are enacted, AI-supported decision processes become
genuinely collaborative in the sense that human actors retain
interpretive authority and relational commitments while leveraging
algorithmic capabilities.

The outcomes associated with ethically managed human-AI
collaboration point toward potential gains in personalization,
professional collaboration, and inclusive school cultures: when human
oversight and value mediation are maintained, AI can enhance
personalized learning and foster new forms of professional cooperation
without displacing empathy, trust, and moral judgment as core
leadership qualities. Reviews of this emerging collaborative order are
careful to note, however, that it remains conceptually vigorous but
empirically preliminary. The broader framework of socio-technical
stewardship — oversight practices, human-in-the-loop decision-
making, value mediation, and formal inspectability tools such as the
Isabelle Infrastructure Framework — is informed by theory and guided
by evidence rather than fully validated, and further in-situ and
longitudinal studies are required to clarify how socio-technical
stewardship can reliably convert ethical intent into sustainable, equity-
oriented practice in AI-mediated schooling.

6.Discussion

6.1. Implications for Policy, Practice, Research, and Governance
6.1.1.Implications for Educational Policy
The evidence synthesized in this review has substantial implications for
educational policy at system, national, and international levels. Perhaps
most urgently, the review underscores the need for coherent,
comprehensive, and democratically legitimate policy frameworks for
AI in school management. The current situation — characterized by
rapid, largely uncoordinated AI adoption driven primarily by vendor
marketing in the absence of robust regulatory oversight — creates
unacceptable risks for students, staff, and communities.
Policy frameworks for AI in school management should, at minimum,
establish: clear requirements for transparency and explainability in AI
systems used in educational settings; robust data protection and privacy
standards that exceed minimum legal requirements and specifically
address the vulnerabilities of students as data subjects; mandatory
ethical impact assessments for AI procurement decisions; requirements
for staff AI literacy training as a condition of AI adoption; community

engagement processes that give families and community members
meaningful voice in AI governance; and regular independent auditing
of AI systems for bias, accuracy, and equity effects.
Beyond these minimum standards, this review suggests that policy
frameworks should actively invest in equity-centered AI development
and deployment, ensuring that the potential benefits of AI in school
management are accessible to under-resourced schools and
communities rather than concentrated in privileged settings. This
requires targeted infrastructure investment, subsidized access to AI
tools for under-resourced schools, capacity-building programs for
educators and administrators in lower-income contexts, and active
engagement with the Global South in the co-design of AI tools for
school management.
6.1.2.Implications for Practice
For school leaders, the review's most important practical implication is
the importance of approaching AI adoption with active rather than
passive engagement. Passive AI adoption — accepting vendor offerings
uncritically, allowing AI systems to automate decisions without
adequate oversight, or treating AI outputs as authoritative — carries
significant risks of the kinds documented throughout this review.
Active AI adoption, by contrast, involves: critical evaluation of AI tools
before procurement; co-design of implementation processes with staff,
students, and families; deliberate cultivation of AI literacy within the
school community; regular critical review of AI-generated outputs
against independent evidence and professional judgment; clear
institutional protocols for human oversight of AI-assisted decisions;
and ongoing reflection on the ethical and equity implications of AI
adoption decisions.
School leaders should also resist the temptation to define AI integration
success primarily in terms of efficiency gains. While efficiency is a
legitimate and valuable benefit, the purpose of school management is
ultimately not efficiency for its own sake but the creation of conditions
in which all students can learn, grow, and flourish. AI tools that enhance
efficiency while undermining equity, trust, or professional culture are
not successful integrations, regardless of their efficiency metrics.
Leaders should evaluate AI adoption in terms of its contribution to the

school's core educational mission, assessed holistically and in dialogue
with all stakeholders.
6.1.3.Implications for Research
The research agenda implied by this review is substantial. The field is
still at an early stage of empirical development: many of the studies
reviewed are theoretical or framework-oriented, empirical studies are
often small-scale, context-specific, and cross-sectional, and the
evidence base for specific claims about AI benefits in school
management is frequently thinner than the enthusiasm of the broader
discourse suggests. A more robust evidence base requires: longitudinal
studies that track the effects of AI integration in school management
over time; large-scale comparative studies across diverse national and
contextual settings; randomized or quasi-experimental designs where
feasible; mixed-methods approaches that combine quantitative outcome
measures with rich qualitative understanding of implementation
processes and stakeholder experiences; systematic attention to equity
and distributional effects; and collaborative research designs that
involve school leaders, teachers, students, families, and communities as
active research partners rather than passive subjects.
The three hypotheses formulated in Section 9 of this review provide a
starting point for this research agenda, but they represent only a fraction
of the empirical questions that remain unanswered. Particularly urgent
are questions about the long-term effects of AI adoption on the
professional identity and judgment of school leaders; the conditions
under which AI tools enhance versus undermine equity in school
management; the mechanisms through which AI literacy affects the
quality of human-AI collaboration in school settings; and the
governance structures that are most effective in ensuring that AI
integration in schools serves democratic and educational values rather
than commercial interests.
6.1.4.Priority actions for Governance and Capacity Building
Converging the technical, ethical, and policy strands of this review into
actionable guidance, five priority actions emerge as a roadmap for
shifting AI use in school management from ad-hoc efficiency gains
toward accountable, educationally aligned practice.

                                Mariana Dogaru

   • Institutionalize governance routines — embed algorithmic audits,
       recurring ethics rounds, and documented human-in-the-loop
       decision points into procurement and day-to-day administrative
       practice.
   • Invest in workforce capacity — provide sustained professional
       learning and budgeted technical support so that principals,
       teachers, and IT staff can interpret, challenge, and override
       system outputs when necessary.
   • Condition vendor relationships on transparency — require data
      portability, audit access, and algorithmic impact assessments
      before deployment of AI tools in high-stakes domains.
   • Adopt technical mitigations where appropriate — federated
      training, differential privacy, and explainable-model toolchains,
      balancing computational costs against usability and equity of
      access.
   • Expand theoretical and empirical work — integrate learning
      science, human-AI collaboration theory, and equity-focused
      frameworks (including critical and intersectional perspectives)
      so that the design and evaluation of AI tools center both
      cognitive mechanisms and socio-political impacts.
Progress on these priorities depends on interdisciplinary collaboration
among educators, technologists, ethicists, and policymakers, and on
empirical programs that trace how leadership choices shape classroom
practice and student outcomes over time. When governance, technical
safeguards, and capacity investments are aligned, AI can support
administrative relief and richer forms of learner support while
respecting privacy, inclusion, and professional judgment.


Conclusions
This expanded systematic review has synthesized the current evidence
on artificial intelligence applications in school management and
organizational support processes, drawing together a systematic
PRISMA-based review, explainability- and ethics-focused literature,
and bibliometric and organizational-management scholarship. The



                                   288

     Artificial Intelligence in School Management. A Systematic Review
                                Mariana Dogaru

picture that emerges is one of genuine and significant potential,
substantial and serious risk, and a field at an early but consequential
stage of development.
The potential is real. AI tools can reduce administrative burden on
school leaders in ways that free time and cognitive resources for the
relational and instructional dimensions of leadership that matter most
for educational quality. AI analytics can surface patterns in complex
educational data that support more evidence-informed, equitable, and
responsive decision-making. AI-powered operational tools can
optimize scheduling, resource allocation, financial and human-resource
management, attendance monitoring, and community communication
in ways that improve school functioning. Predictive and generative
technologies promise administrative efficiencies and new modes of
personalization. These benefits are not hypothetical; they are
documented across multiple national contexts, organizational functions,
and study types.
The risks are equally real. Algorithmic bias can reproduce and
legitimize discriminatory patterns. Data privacy and cybersecurity
breaches can harm vulnerable students and families. Over-reliance on
AI can erode the professional judgment, relational intelligence, and
contextual wisdom that are the irreducible core of educational
leadership. Digital divides and interoperability gaps can concentrate AI
benefits in already-privileged settings. Policy vacuums can allow
vendor lock-in and commercial interests to shape AI adoption in
schools without adequate democratic accountability. Across all of these
domains, three persistent tensions recur: the trade-off between
predictive performance and transparency, the distribution of authority
between automated systems and human decision-makers, and the
ethical stewardship of sensitive student data.
The resolution of this tension — between potential and risk — is not a
technical but a human and political project. It requires educational
leaders who approach AI with critical literacy, ethical commitment, and
democratic accountability — leaders who, in the language of Principal
Ethical AI Leadership, act as socio-technical stewards aligning
operational uses of AI with professional norms, equity commitments,
and legal obligations. It requires policymakers who design governance

                                Mariana Dogaru

frameworks that protect students and communities while enabling
responsible innovation, and who condition vendor relationships on
transparency, data portability, and audit access. It requires researchers
who generate the rigorous, equity-centered evidence base that
responsible AI integration in schools demands, moving beyond
technology-acceptance studies toward designs that trace leadership
decisions to classroom practices, student learning, and equity outcomes.
And it requires the voices of students, teachers, families, and
communities to be genuinely heard in decisions about how AI is
adopted, governed, and evaluated in educational organizations.
The central argument of this review is that AI in school management is
neither a solution nor a threat, but a set of tools whose effects depend
entirely on the wisdom, values, and intentions of the humans who
deploy them. Its value lies in augmenting, not replacing, wise human
leadership committed to educational justice. Educational leaders who
engage with AI thoughtfully, critically, and with an unwavering
commitment to educational justice have genuine reason for optimism.
Those who adopt AI passively, uncritically, or primarily in service of
efficiency have reason for serious concern. The difference lies not in
the technology, but in the quality of human leadership that surrounds it.
This review calls for continued, rigorous, and equity-centered research;
for robust and democratically legitimate policy frameworks; for
governance routines that embed algorithmic audits and ethics rounds
into everyday practice; and for a profession-wide commitment to
developing the AI literacy, ethical reasoning capacity, and reflective
practice that responsible AI integration in school management
demands. The future of AI in schools will be made, not given — and it
will be made by the choices that educators, leaders, researchers, and
communities make now.
