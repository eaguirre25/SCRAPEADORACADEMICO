   SUMMARY
   The study examines how trust is manifested in principals’ co-operation with their own
   employees in the implementation of local development work. The research question is:
   How is trust expressed in principals’ co-operation with employees and leaders? The study is
   based on interviews with three principals in a medium-sized municipality. Key findings
   indicate that trust is illustrated through the recruitment and selection of employees,
   professional leadership, employee participation, and reputation building. The findings
   are discussed using theories of co-operation, trust, agency, governance and leadership.

   Keywords: interaction, trust, agency, leadership



Introduction
Trust is supposedly lacking in many public institutions, such as schools (Boin &
Christensen, 2008; Høyer & Wood, 2011). The government’s trust reform aims to
increase employees’ professional freedom and make schools more responsive
to experiences and viewpoints within the local community. The reform also aims to
strengthen the dimension of trust among school leaders and school owners (Ministry
of Local Government and Regional Development, 2022). One challenge is that there
is little systematic research on the significance of trust in the co-operation between
school leaders and teachers (van Maele & van Houtte, 2009). Without solid knowledge,
the ability to know what school leaders can do to strengthen trust and counteract mis-
trust in co-operation is weakened. This study will examine primarily co-operation
between principals, their own teachers, and other leaders in the school, but also co-
operation with the school owner. The implementation of school-specific development
plans is a key reference point when examining the significance of trust. Data collection

Theoretical framework
Co-operation and agency
In this study, co-operation is defined as ‘an open and equal communication and devel-
opment process between actors who complement each other in terms of competence
and exchange expertise, either directly face to face or mediated via technology or man-
ually, working towards a common goal, and where the relationship between the actors
at all times relies on trust, involvement, rationality, or industry knowledge’ (Torgersen
& Steiro, 2009, p. 153). The term agency is used to refer to the opportunities individu-
als have to determine the content, method, and evaluation of their own efforts (Helleve
et al., 2018). Autonomy is the core of the concept of agency and is anchored in a range
of principles and laws, both national and international (Paulsrud & Wermke, 2020).
Understanding agency depends on previous experiences, as well as knowledge and atti-
tudes activated in specific situations (Biesta, 2017). Agency is not an inherent attribute or
competence that people initially possess, but a capacity they develop through their actions
(Frigstad & Gjems, 2023). For example, principals will, to a greater or lesser extent, ena-
ble teachers to make their own professional choices (Espedal, 2006). However, there is
variation in how people interpret, adapt to, or resist guidelines within an organisation. If
many detailed requirements for predetermined structures and given actions are imposed,
it will reduce teachers’ opportunities to make academically based decisions (Frigstad &
Gjems, 2023). For example, the school owner imposing specific educational programmes
on schools entails a narrowing of agency for both principals and teachers. Conversely,
the school owner can also expand the agency of principals, trusting that employees are
capable of prioritising and making good and well-founded choices.


Trust reduces complexity
According to Grimen, there is no definition of the concept of trust that is not con-
tested. Also, trust is difficult to separate from related concepts, such as confidence and
familiarity (Grimen, 2009; Schilke et al., 2021). Trust is described as a prerequisite for
establishing and engaging in relationships between people, and between people and
institutions (Kristiansen, 2005). Grimen (2009) describes trust as the glue, lubricant,
and foundation of society. His perception is that people who trust each other co-
operate more, are more tolerant, are willing to take risks, and are creative and sat-
isfied. Trust also functions as a complexity-reducing mechanism (Luhmann et al.,
2017; Kristiansen, 2005). That means that trust simplifies, makes life manageable, and
provides a sense of control. Cummings and Bromily (1996) further argue that trust
contributes to reducing unnecessary energy loss within and between organisations.
According to Hoy and Morani (1999), trust will help reduce uncertainty and maintain
order within the organisation.

Trust presupposes willingness to take risks
Bentzen defines trust as ‘a psychological state in which one actor chooses to make
themselves vulnerable to another, knowing that there is no guarantee that the other
will live up to that trust’ (2018, p. 27). The one who gives trust consciously takes few
or no precautions (Grimen, 2009). Trust is also considered a prerequisite for collab-
oration and innovation in organisations, since it creates more complex possibilities
for action (Bentzen, 2018). Since trust never implies full certainty, it quickly becomes
a matter of risk, and willingness to take risks (Bentzen, 2018; Luhmann et al., 2017).
In fact, Burns (Burns & Cerna, 2016) points out that trust is not about taking risks, but
about the willingness to take risks. He refutes the idea that it is possible to determine
whether risk precedes trust or is a result of it, but argues that avoiding risk will result
in problems remaining unresolved. A school is therefore dependent on the existence of
a willingness to take risks on the part of the actors (Luhmann et al., 2017; Kristiansen,
2005), especially in planning the school’s activities, where some factors will neces-
sarily be unknown or uncertain. According to Luhmann et al. (2017) there will always
be a risk of errors being made. Burns et al. (2016) emphasise that it will be a leader’s
task to encourage and provide feedback that promotes development and innovation.
The premise is that trust makes collaboration easier. The risk of giving trust is that it
may be reciprocated by distrust, for example, by employees failing to disclose mat-
ters that put themselves or others in an unfavourable light, such as the progress of a
development project coming to a halt. Mistrust is often perceived as the opposite of
trust and can entail significant costs since it requires vigilance from the principal’s
side (Grimen, 2009). For example, there might be silent protests or active attempts
to undermine the principal’s plans or decisions. However, distrust can also lead to
innovations and contribute to employees not blindly developing trust in each other
(Bentzen, 2018). Distrust can lead to voices being heard, taken seriously, and thus used
in the development of the school.


Trust in interaction
Trust can reduce uncertainty by creating a form of order in the organisation,
thus reducing the possibility of being paralysed by inaction (Kristiansen, 2005).
Furthermore, agency enables building upon each other’s efforts (Grimen, 2009).
There is little knowledge about how differences in power, sanctioning capabilities,
and communication methods affect co-operation between those who give and those
who receive trust (Bormann et al., 2022). According to Burns et al. (2016), too much
trust can result in distrust, because uncritical trust can be exploited. Showing trust
is portrayed as the opposite of holding someone accountable (Ehren et al., 2020).
But trust can also be a prerequisite for increased accountability leading to change or
development. Nevertheless, external control can reduce agency and development in
an environment where there is already a lot of mistrust, while more trust can promote
employee responsibility and participation (Ehren et al., 2020). Bormann et al. (2022)
distinguish between trust and acts of trust. Collaborating, delegating responsibility, or

seeking advice are all actions that presuppose trust, and demonstrate a willingness to
take risks. Trust contributes to employees making greater efforts to fulfil their obli-
gations, and to behave honestly and selflessly in situations that could otherwise be
exploited for personal gain. Furthermore, trust can be an expression of expectations
shared by employees in a school. ‘Trust is a generalized expectancy held by the work
group that the word, promise, and written or oral statement of another individual,
group, or organization can be relied upon’ (Rotter, 1967; cited in Forsyth et al., 2011,
p. 4). Trust is here described at three different levels: trust in the principal, trust in
colleagues, and trust in pupils. Trust consists of each level being regarded as reliable,
and all of them acting in the best interests of the school. Transparency is considered
the most important single factor for the school’s staff to have trust in the principal
(Tschannen-Moran, 2001).


Employee participation, governance and leadership
Effective principals practise transparency and employee participation. This is done
through processes that ensure co-operation with employees. At the same time, teach-
ers demand greater influence on decisions being made (Tschannen-Moran, 2001).
According to van Maele et al. (2009), research on trust has largely been orientated
towards, and measured against, the functionality and effectiveness of schools, focus-
ing on trust emerging in collective processes, and among groups of actors such as
teacher groups. Since trust is associated with better functionality in organisations,
it is reasonable to assume that it also has a positive impact on schools, even though
schools have their peculiarities compared to other organisations (Bryk & Schneider,
2002; Kochanek, 2005). According to van Maele et al. (2009), trust is, to a large extent,
based on the values, size, and organisation of the school. The researchers conclude
that principals should be mindful of their leadership style, since it is more important
than organisational factors. Trust is considered a key factor in establishing good rela-
tionships and effective leadership (Forsyth et al., 2011).
   Employees represent a significant resource that requires management through dis-
tributing responsibilities and tasks (Bentzen, 2018; Johnsen et al., 2022). Governance
involves using available tools and resources to achieve the school’s goals. An important
consideration is to balance local variations with the ability to uphold overarching goals
(Burns & Cerna, 2016). Leadership involves making decisions and dealing with inter-
personal relationships in formal and informal situations (Bentzen, 2018; Johnsen et al.,
2022). According to Bentzen (2018), the interaction and balance between governance
and leadership are vital for development to occur. How principals communicate on a
daily basis is also important. By encouraging and supporting professional engagement
and development, trust can be developed (Benzen, 2018). Over time, frequent pres-
ence can help build collective expectations (van Male et al., 2009), and reduce energy
loss (Cummings & Bromily, 1996). It can help reduce any uncertainty and maintain
order and predictability (Hoy & Morani, 1999). Trust presupposes willingness to take
risks, facilitates co-operation and collaboration, and reduces the need for control and

monitoring (Levi, 1998). The term ‘trust-based governance and leadership’ is used to
show that governance and leadership are interconnected in practice (Bentzen, 2018).
Trust can be crucial in achieving a balance between conflicting interests or goals, and
it is closely linked to power and its exercise (Burns et al., 2016). Granting trust enables
agency for employees to make autonomous and professional choices (Espedal, 2006).
It is expected that the one given trust will use it in accordance with the interests of the
trust giver (Grimen, 2009).
   Leadership is clearly linked to the principal`s exercise of power. If this is to be per-
ceived as legitimate, it will be crucial that the principal establishes rules and follows
them himself. (Luhmann, 1979; Møller, 2006; Sørhaug, 2004). In schools, power is
asymmetric. This means that the principal controls access to resources for pupils,
staff, parents, and the school owner alike, thereby setting the framework for co-
operation. According to Grimen (2009), trusting employees can entail the exercise
of power by employees in several areas, but without the trust giver necessarily being
aware of it. It is also difficult to distinguish clearly between actions based on trust, and
actions based on coercion. As long as a leader follows established norms and rules, the
position of power is perceived as legitimate (Sørhaug, 2004).
   A central consideration will be the preservation of trust and agency that teachers are
given through the curriculum, and by virtue of being a profession (Hopmann, 2015). It
will be crucial to trust employees so that they can be autonomous (Klijn et al., 1997;
Nyhan, 2000). Since reality is both complex and changing, it would be impractical to
provide specific instructions on how tasks should be accomplished. Trust becomes a
prerequisite for practicing autonomy and ensuring necessary flexibility for employ-
ees. The challenge lies in helping employees become capable of finding good solutions
(Sørensen, 2007). The principal not providing such assistance, could be perceived as an
abdication of responsibility. According to Burns et al. (2016), it is possible to combine
empowering employees and trust, but this requires balance. According to Bormann
et al. (2022), reforms and development work require change on multiple levels: in the
classroom, in the school as an organisation, and on the level of the school owner as
the responsible authority. Development largely depends on the support provided, the
measures taken to reduce resistance to change, and what is done to empower employ-
ees and enable them to utilise methods and external support systems. Sustained devel-
opment work also requires the principal to limit their control, because without trust,
employees will feel that they only have themselves to rely on (Bormann et al., 2022).



Method
Interview
The purpose of the interviews is to obtain comprehensive and descriptive information
about how the informants express and experience trust in their interactions with their
own employees and leaders. Individual interviews were conducted with three princi-
pals at the primary and lower secondary levels in a medium-sized municipality. The
purpose was to elicit experiences and reflections related to trust in the informants’




                                           169

                                    Trond Frigstad


co-operation with their own employees and the school owner (Christoffersen &
Johannessen, 2012). The interviews were conducted individually, since the pur-
pose was to highlight a diversity of practices. The interviews were semi-structured
and guided by an interview guide. The guide is inspired by theoretical perspectives,
which have been presented, and contains questions about the informants’ strategies
and experiences with trust in their co-operation with their own employees and the
school owner. Follow-up questions were asked to ensure that the topic was explored
in a comprehensive manner. The duration of the interviews was 45-60 minutes. Audio
recordings were made, which were then transcribed and checked in full text, and the
text serves as the basis for the analysis (Elo & Kyngäs, 2007). The study is approved by
the Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research (SIKT).


Selection
A strategic selection was made, meaning that the informants represent the group of
principals in the municipality that was studied. All the principals were asked in writing
to participate, and three chose to do so. They were distributed across the primary and
lower secondary levels, and all were participating in a multi-year leadership devel-
opment programme in the municipality, where the implementation of the schools’
development plans is a central theme. The informants all have extensive experience
as principals (9–15 years). They lead schools of similar size, one primary school and
two lower secondary schools, and they share a commitment to academic and organi-
sational development. They are referred to as Informant 1, Informant 2, and Informant
3. The group of leaders includes inspectors and department heads. Employees refers to
teachers, although the school also has employees who are unqualified, and profes-
sionals who are not trained teachers.


Analysis
After the interviews were conducted, they were transcribed. The text was thoroughly
reviewed to validate and document the informants’ statements (Hsieh & Shannon,
2005). To detect themes and patterns in the data, an inductive approach was chosen
(Berg & Lune, 2015). It turned out that the informants had varying degrees of close-
ness to professional management in the development work, and they placed differing
emphasis on the control and governance of employees. The next step was to iden-
tify statements that could be linked to how the principals practise trust. Based on the
statements, a condensed and clear presentation of the main statements was devel-
oped. These were then placed into the following categories: recruitment and selection;
professional management and HR; employee participation and reputation building.
The categories were used to identify similarities and differences in the material. The
analysis is my interpretation of what the informants have stated. The process began
when the interviews were conducted and has continued until completion. The results
are presented and discussed using relevant theory so as to be as transparent and bal-
anced as possible.

Limitations of the study and research ethics considerations
Questions may arise about a sample that is limited to three informants. The number
of informants, combined with their employment in the same municipality, may also
raise concerns that their statements could be traced. Furthermore, the responses may
have been influenced by the informants’ desire to present themselves in a favourable
light, for example, to the school owner or to me as the researcher. The findings cannot
be generalised beyond the sample, and the results must therefore be interpreted with
caution. My interpretation, along with any theoretical framework, will entail limita-
tions and could lead to important findings being overlooked (Fauskanger & Mosvold,
2014). It is also possible that I have not been sufficiently inquisitive and open during the
interview situation or in my interpretation of results (Christoffersen & Johannessen,
2012). The informants were made aware before, during, and after the interviews that
they would be anonymised and that they could withdraw at any time. They were also
given the opportunity to review the transcribed text but declined.



Results
Recruitment and selection of employees
The informants are concerned with being able to convey trust by recruiting leaders
with the right motivation and prerequisites for development work:

    I quickly noticed who could be a development leader (Informant 1).

Similarly, teachers who want to commit themselves to development tasks are given
a choice. In addition, the informants facilitate activities that involve all employees in
processes that entail responsibility and personal development:

    I show trust by empowering the employees, supporting them, and allowing
    them to try and fail (Informant 3).



Professional leadership
The principals trust their leaders by delegating responsibility for difficult conversa-
tions with employees. In addition, they are given responsibility for leading profes-
sional processes within the teacher group. Since the outcome of such division of work
will be unclear, there is a risk associated with delegation:

    Then I’m on slightly uncertain ground myself, because maybe it won’t turn out
    exactly as I thought (Informant 3).

Another informant is more concerned with retaining control, including by facilitating
professional activities:

    It’s my task to plan the development time to be so exciting and educational
    that the teachers don’t refrain from coming. I also try to show them where we
    are heading and that everything is interconnected (Informant 2).

The informant points out that trust and credibility are built through continuous

    Trust means that they know who I am and what I stand for, so now they listen

The informant also emphasises that employees can choose to respond to expectations

    What is the leadership up to now (suspicious)?


Or it could be:

    What do they mean by that statement (interested)? (Informant 2).


The established systems for operation and education in the school and municipality
pose challenges when they need to be followed up:

    We also create systems where we can help each other, but it’s easier said than
    done (Informant 2).


The informant emphasises that if you are to become good at leading an organisation in
development mode, you must establish some ways of working that are contagious. One
example is Open Space methodology:

    It’s about the big team and that everyone agrees on the values. The employees
    received it very well (Informant 2).

In addition, emphasis is placed on being a good role model so that both inspectors and
team leaders instil trust in the teachers. In line with this, one informant states:

    We practice very distributed leadership, and then people must have both
    tasks and responsibilities, as well as the entire portfolio, otherwise it’s not fun
    (Informant 1).

At the same time, the informant states:

    As a principal, I have managerial authority over two-thirds of the employees’
    working hours, and then I can say that you should engage in development
    work (Informant 1).

The same informant states that they are more focused on providing a reality check to

Employee participation
The informants are concerned with employees’ opportunities to co-create, partic-
ipate, and collaborate. Informant 3 states that this is something they pay particular
attention to in shadowing, which involves closely following up on employees in their
everyday work. A strategic move is to entrust the employees with operationalising
the overarching development plan from the school owner. Informant 3 believes that
through involvement and responsibility, the employees become more like pilots in
their own development work. The idea is that by giving employees trust, you receive
trust in return. This also entails the opportunity to try and fail. The same inform-
ant states that whilst exercising leadership, they monitor and control very little,
but adds:

    Of course, it’s a form of control that everyone should create their own devel-
    opment plans (Informant 3).


Another example is given of how the person controls the employees:

    As a principal, I have managerial authority over two-thirds of the employees’
    working hours, and then I can say that you should engage in development
    work (Informant 1).


A fundamental requirement for the informants to gain trust is that the leadership
(both leaders and principals) have the prerequisites to address challenges among the
staff. One way to do this is for the leaders to practise together, and have several people
involved in conducting challenging staff discussions. According to one of the inform-
ants, this works.

    At times, employees may disagree with assessments and conclusions, but they
    still take constructive criticism onboard (Informant 2).


One informant also points out the necessity of having trust in one’s own judgments
and not waiting for input or suggestions from one’s own employees (Informant 1).
Another informant is not averse to asking for help:

    I’m not afraid to ask the other leaders for help (Informant 2).

In addition, employees are involved in decisions about the school’s priorities. One
example is when the school owner mandates schools to start a new initiative, but the
school is busy with implementing established plans:

    One year, the school owner decided that we should start reading loops. But
    it was decided quite late. There were good arguments for working with the
    pupils’ reading skills. But we had already made other plans, and the majority
    of teachers decided that we should stick to these and not start anything new
    (Informant 2).


In cases like this, the informant discusses with the employees whether the school
should follow the directives of the school owner or complete what they have already
started. The decision is, as shown, left to the majority. A well-functioning operation is
mentioned as crucial in order to focus the employees’ attention on development work.
It involves eliminating time-wasters and disturbances, and creating the basis, sur-
plus, and motivation for development. For example, the importance of having a func-
tioning substitute system is mentioned (Informant 1).


Reputation and trust
Conscious reputation building within the municipality is mentioned as important for
the school to maintain the necessary trust within the local community (Informants 1
and 2). One experience is that a good reputation can hinder development in the school
as the school’s employees cling to their reputation, and do not pay sufficient attention
to what can further develop the school.

    The employees thought that the reputation was good, but this was not true at
    all. They themselves think they were so good and weren`t interested in chang-
    ing. The key for me was to find a good way to tell them, while at the same time
    it was important to include what did work well (Informant 2).


The informants also indicate that trust has significance beyond the individual school,
such as trust in the school owner:

    I like that the school owner sets expectations for me – that I deliver
    (Informant 1).


At the same time, there is understanding and acceptance from the school owner that
leadership should be entrusted to the principals, and that this entails both responsi-
bility and authority. One informant has experienced being unnecessarily instructed
and clarifies:

     I need to work with my people in a way that is wise (Informant 2).


Generally, there is trust that various initiatives from the school owner have their
justification:

    It’s rare to receive a school initiative that is completely out of place. I haven’t

The informants also trust that national authorities and competence environments

Discussion
In this section, the results are discussed in light of the presented theory of interaction,
trust, management, and leadership.
   The results show that one way to build trust is by recruiting teachers and lead-
ers with interest and potential for development. They both represent a significant
resource that requires governance through the distribution of responsibilities and
tasks (Bentzen, 2018; Johnsen et al., 2022). How principals communicate on a daily
basis is also of great importance. By encouraging and supporting professional com-
mitment and development, trust can be developed (Benzen, 2018). This is done by pri-
oritising presence in formal and informal arenas of the school. Over time, frequent
presence can contribute to building up collective expectations about how and how
often the principal is present (van Maele et al., 2009). A practice that involves clear
presence can reduce energy loss (Cummings & Bromily, 1996), and lead to a reduction
in uncertainty, and the maintenance of order and predictability (Hoy & Morani, 1999).
The level of involvement of the informants themselves varies in terms of the planning,
implementation, and evaluation of development work. One of the informants actively
participates in the development group (Informant 2), while the other two (Informants
1 and 3) have delegated responsibility to other leaders and teachers. Delegation can
be perceived as a statement of trust intended to promote employee autonomy and
self-management (Bormann et al., 2022). For the informants to succeed, it is presup-
posed that employees are able to solve their own tasks (Sørensen, 2007). One of the
informants states that it is not necessary to control and follow up on employees work-
ing with development. This might be interpreted as a lack of interest in identifying and
addressing the needs of the employees. But it can also indicate that the informant is
willing to take risks (Burns, 2016), even when it means that goals or ambitions may
not be fulfilled. According to Burns (2016), it will be difficult to clarify whether the
risk is taken prior to the responsibility being delegated, or is a result of trust built up
before delegating responsibility. The central point seems to be that trust can also be
a prerequisite for increased accountability leading to change or development (Ehren
et al, 2020). According to Leithwood and Steinbach (1995), the informants involve
both leaders and teachers in the school’s processes. For example, the leaders conduct
challenging staff discussions, and they practice, plan, and conduct these discussions

together. Informant 2 describes the experiences as positive, since the teachers feel
they are met with respect and trust (Luhmann et al., 2017). When the principal also
chooses to be extensively involved in challenging staff discussions, it can be under-
stood as a lack of willingness to take risks by delegating a larger portion of the respon-
sibility to their own leaders (Burns & Cerna, 2016; Benzten, 2018). On the one hand,
the principal`s participation can be explained as a genuine interest in and experience
in collaborating on demanding personnel issues. On the other hand, it can also express
a lack of trust that the school`s other leaders can handle this on their own.
   According to Informant 2, teachers feel as if they have been taken into considera-
tion, even though the topics can be demanding and conversations do not always go as
planned (Luhmann et al., 2017). It has not been revealed whether teachers and lead-
ers have a real influence on the decisions made, or if participation is limited to being
involved in preparatory processes (Bacharach et al., 1989).
   One explanation for delegating development responsibilities and tasks could be
a conscious prioritisation of operational tasks, such as ensuring that substitute sys-
tems function and eliminating time-wasters. This can lead to employees having their
capacity freed up to pursue development. The informant’s own explanation is that
involvement is not necessary, because they have such great trust in the employees. An
alternative explanation could be that facilitation, through well-functioning operations,
may increase the risk of development processes stalling or being delayed. This could
mean that the informant does not want to take this risk. But it could also mean that the
informant is balancing responsibility and trust in a suitable manner (Burns et al., 2016).
   Even though the responsibility for carrying out development processes is delegated,
one of the informants chooses to be present frequently at formal meetings, through
digital platforms, and in informal settings. Such presence will be a way to demon-
strate and build trust. But the informant (Informant 3) points out that it is also a way
of exerting control, an approach that illustrates the balance between governance and
leadership (Bentzen, 2018). In this case, a precaution is introduced, which may mean
that limitations are placed on employees’ insight into what is happening within the
professional community (Grimen, 2009). It is reasonable to assume that the bound-
aries between what constitutes governance and leadership can be unclear and fluid,
depending on what the informants aim to achieve. Leadership is undeniably linked to
the principal’s exercise of power. If this is to be perceived as legitimate, it will be cru-
cial for the principal to establish the rules of the game and adhere to them (Luhmann,
1979; Møller, 2006; Sørhaug, 2004;). It may be that the informants (Informants 1 and
3) largely choose to delegate responsibility to employees because it is sensible in terms
of resources (Bentzen, 2018; Johnsen et al., 2022), in line with their own interests
(Grimen, 2009; Skedsmo et al., 2017), or because it coincides with the expectations
the teachers themselves have for their own participation. On the other hand, it cannot
be ruled out that the active presence of the informants reflects acceptance and trust
from the employees, based on the belief that common interests are being taken care
of through complementary competencies (Torgersen & Steiro, 2009). It may also be

better success in advocating for their interests by having their ‘hands on the wheel’
(Grimen, 2009). One experience is that close and open co-operation also makes it pos-

   It also emerges that the school owner is eager to promote proposals for new focus
areas, but these sometimes clash with the school’s own development plans. In such
cases, the situation is discussed internally at the school, and the majority decides what
the school will do (Informant 3). Without an internal process at the school, the inform-
ant risks being responsible for decisions that are not sufficiently rooted in their own
organisation. There can also be risks associated with a minority not aligning with the
majority (Grimen, 2009), either because the decision does not consider the agency
teachers are expected to have (Helleve et al., 2018; Paulsrud & Wermke, 2020), or because
it conflicts with existing values (van Maele et al., 2009) or expectations (Forsyth et al.,
2011). There will also be a risk associated with decisions going against the expecta-
tions and directives of the school owner. Both scenarios could lead to a weakened rep-
utation and decrease in trust within the local community, among employees, or from
the school owner. Even though decisions may entail a negative reputation in the short
term, they can still lead to growth, development, and trust over time (Frigstad, 2023).
The informants in this study problematise to a small extent how resistance to deci-
sion-making can affect trust in relationships over time, both internally and externally.
   One of the informants exercises governance by determining how employees should
spend their time. Without doing so, there is a risk that tasks will not be completed
(Grimen, 2009). However, there is also a risk of losing trust from employees, if they
perceive that decisions disregard their autonomy and agency (Paulsrud & Wermke,
2020), and if there is no transparency around the decisions made (Forsyth et al., 2011).
On the other hand, majority decisions could also be interpreted as a lack of precau-
tions against democratic decision-making processes (Grimen, 2009). An argument
would be that even though the minority may feel sidelined, there are established rules
that are predictable and effective, contributing to legitimacy and trust. The extent to
which there is consensus on the school`s rules is uncertain, but it cannot be ruled out
that they are perceived and practiced somewhat differently. In a conflict between the
school’s priorities and the school owner’s expectations, what the principal chooses
to do will be significant for the trust of both employees and the school owner. The

Main findings and the way forward
The purpose of the study has been to gain new knowledge about how principals and
schools practice and build trust in the implementation of their own school’s develop-
ment plans. The findings indicate that the informants make different choices, and this
affects how they experience and practise trust in their co-operation with employees
and the school owner. The research question was: How is trust expressed in the principal’s
co-operation with their own employees and the school owner? The findings document that
trust is a central element in the principal’s co-operation and encompasses both govern-
ance and leadership. The informants practise trust through recruitment and selection
of employees, professional management, HR, employee participation, and reputation
building. These are topics that can serve as guides and frameworks for school leaders,
who seek awareness of their own position and dialogue about what could be good choices
to strengthen trust in co-operation between people. Themes for further research could
include investigating the significance of trust for pupils’ learning environments and
well-being. In addition, it will be relevant to study how trust influences co-operation
between sectors and across administrative levels. For example, is there a correlation
between trust and services provided to children and youth within and outside of school?
The same question can be investigated in light of the potential impact that mistrust may
have, both on the individual and group levels. It would also be relevant to investigate
whether high levels of trust in an organisation can result in increased mistrust (Burns
et al., 2016), and whether excessive trust can lead to dependency and cognitive disen-
gagement, as some have argued (Bormann et al., 2022; Schilke et al., 2021).
