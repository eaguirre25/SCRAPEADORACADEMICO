                                      Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into educational systems
has rapidly transitioned from speculative innovation to an operational
imperative, driven by global investments projected to exceed $20
billion by 2027. Schools worldwide now face a critical juncture:
algorithmic systems are increasingly embedded in administrative
workflows, pedagogical personalisation, and strategic decision-
making (Van Slyke et al., 2023). Early adopters showcase AI’s potential
to optimise scheduling, predict dropout risks, and tailor learning
pathways- advancements that promise to revolutionise education
(Androniceanu, 2024; Rajalakshmi, 2024). However, these successes
remain fragmented, with limited synthesised evidence to guide
scalable and equitable implementation across diverse institutional
contexts. This systematic review addresses this gap by analysing 16
peer-reviewed studies (2020–2024) to construct an integrative
framework for AI deployment in school management, balancing
efficiency, equity, and sustainability.

AI’s penetration into education manifests through three primary
vectors: administrative automation, adaptive learning systems, and
predictive analytics. Administrative automation streamlines tasks
such as scheduling and resource allocation, freeing educators to focus
on instruction. Adaptive learning platforms personalise content
delivery by analysing student performance data, while predictive
analytics identify at-risk learners for early intervention (Huang, 2022).
Case studies illustrate AI’s dual role as an efficiency driver and equity
lever.   For     instance,       Vietnamese          schools       automated          34%         of
administrative tasks using AI, while early warning systems in Greece
reduced rural dropout rates by 18% (Avraam, 2023; Nouri, 2024). Yet
these achievements are often siloed within specific regions or tools,

failing to account for systemic barriers like infrastructural inequities.
In Sub-Saharan Africa, 63% of schools lack reliable internet access,
starkly limiting AI’s reach and reinforcing global educational divides
(Nuryana, 2024; Leong, 2024). Similarly, while AI reduces teacher
workloads by 11.2 hours weekly in high-resource settings,
underprepared systems face unintended consequences, such as
increased burnout from added technical demands (Rosi, 2024;
Sugiarso, 2024).

These disparities underscore a central paradox in AI adoption: its
transformative potential is tempered by unresolved tensions between
efficiency and equity, scalability and personalisation, and innovation
and governance (Mafi, 2023; Pörn, 2024). First, efficiency-equity
tradeoffs reveal that automated systems, despite 92% accuracy in
student placement algorithms, often replicate socioeconomic biases
when trained on historically skewed datasets (Quyet, 2020a; Quyet,
2024b). Second, the scalability-personalisation dilemma highlights the
challenge of designing AI tools that function effectively across diverse
contexts. For example, natural language processing tutors improved
language acquisition by 23% in Algeria but faltered in multicultural
classrooms where dialectal diversity required nuanced adaptation
(Djouiba, 2024). Third, the innovation-governance gap exposes
regulatory shortcomings: 78% of schools lack AI ethics committees,
leaving real-time analytics tools operating in a void of accountability
(Hammoumi, 2024; Huang et al., 2020). These contradictions illustrate
the inadequacy of tool-centric approaches and emphasise the need for
systemic frameworks that align technological capabilities with
organisational readiness, policy infrastructure, and ethical oversight
(Roberts, 2024).

Much of the existing literature on AI in education focuses on its
pedagogical dimensions, adaptive learning, intelligent tutoring,
student engagement, leaving its administrative and governance
dimensions      comparatively           underexplored.            Systematic         reviews
published before 2024 have tended to catalogue AI tools by function
rather than examining how they interact with school organisational
structures, leadership cultures, or institutional readiness. Theoretical
connections between AI deployment and established frameworks in
educational management, such as distributed leadership, change
management,          or      socio-technical           theory,         remain         largely
underdeveloped. This absence limits practitioners’ ability to plan AI
adoption as an organisational process rather than a technical
installation. The present review departs from prior work by situating
AI applications within a school management and leadership context,
synthesising evidence across resource-diverse settings, and generating
governance-oriented guidance that speaks directly to administrators
and policymakers rather than technologists alone.

This study transcends isolated case analyses to address two pivotal
questions: (1) How can AI applications be strategically mapped across
administrative, pedagogical, and leadership functions to maximise
synergies?     (2)     What       evidence-based             interventions          mitigate
implementation barriers related to infrastructure, data governance,
and human capital? Through thematic synthesis of 2,143 data points,
the review advances three novel contributions. First, it develops a
tiered implementation framework that aligns AI tools with
institutional maturity levels, offering a roadmap for schools at
different stages of technological readiness. Second, it quantifies the
risk-reward ratios of predictive analytics across high- and low-
resource contexts, identifying contexts where AI yields the greatest

impact and where caution is warranted. Third, it proposes a global
competency matrix for AI-ready school leadership, outlining the skills
and governance structures necessary to steward ethical and effective
AI integration.

The urgency of these contributions is amplified by the global shift
toward mandated AI adoption. Over 84% of OECD nations now
include AI integration in national education strategies, reflecting its
growing centrality to educational innovation. However, without
systemic     frameworks,          schools       risk      exacerbating   inequities,
misallocating resources, and undermining teacher agency. This review
equips policymakers and administrators with actionable strategies to
navigate these challenges, ensuring AI’s benefits are harnessed
equitably.

                                Research Method

This study employs a systematic review with thematic synthesis,
structured according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for
Systematic      Reviews          and       Meta‑Analyses)           framework,   to
comprehensively map AI applications in K–12 school management
(Page et al., 2021; Bhattarai, 2024). Unlike statistical meta-analysis,
which aggregates numerical effect sizes across studies, thematic
synthesis integrates qualitative and mixed-methods findings by
identifying patterns of meaning across a body of literature, an
approach well suited to the heterogeneous designs and contextual
variability of studies in this field (Thomas & Harden, 2008). The
researcher deliberately confined the review to peer‑reviewed articles
published between January 2020 and December 2024, a period that
encompasses both the introduction of transformative generative AI
models—such as OpenAI’s GPT‑3 (May 2020) and ChatGPT

(November 2022) (Neumann, 2023; Slyke, 2023; Liu et al. 2024), and the
accelerated adoption of digital and AI‑driven educational solutions
during the COVID‑19 pandemic (Tan, 2023). This temporal boundary
also aligns with a marked surge in scholarly output on AI in education
(Liu, 2024; Chukwubueze, 2024), ensuring that the analysis reflects the
most recent technological breakthroughs and their implications for
school administration.

The review unfolds over four sequential phases—search strategy,
study selection, quality appraisal, and data synthesis—each designed
to address methodological rigour and to fill gaps noted in the
introduction. By adhering to the 27-item PRISMA checklist, the
researcher documents every decision from initial identification to final
inclusion, facilitating an audit trail that other researchers can follow or
replicate (Figure 1).

The search strategy targeted three major bibliographic databases,
Scopus, Web of Science, and IEEE Xplore, chosen for their extensive
coverage of education, technology, and interdisciplinary studies. The
researcher constructed a Boolean string combining AI-related terms
“artificial intelligence,” “machine learning” with school management
concepts “K–12 administration,” “educational leadership”, and
functional    outcomes          “administrative            efficiency,”         “predictive
analytics,” “adaptive learning,” “resource allocation”. To capture the
rapid evolution of AI, particularly after the release of GPT-3 and in the
context of pandemic‑driven digital transformation, the search was
limited to peer‑reviewed articles published between January 2020 and
December 2024. No language filters were applied initially; however,
non‑English articles lacking accessible translations were excluded
during the screening phase to maintain consistency in interpretation.

The initial search produced 142 records, which were imported into
EndNote       for   deduplication.          Using      EndNote’s          built‑in   “Find
Duplicates” feature and manual cross‑verification, 30 duplicate entries
were removed, resulting in 112 unique items for further screening. This
semi‑automated approach streamlined the deduplication process
while ensuring accuracy, although recent studies suggest that
specialised tools like the Deduplicator can further reduce time and
error rates in record removal (Forbes et al., 2024; Hutson et al., 2022).

Next, two independent reviewers conducted title and abstract
screening against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.
Inclusion      criteria      mandated           empirical            studies—qualitative,
quantitative, or mixed‑methods—focused explicitly on AI in K–12
school management and peer‑reviewed in Scopus, Web of Science, or
PubMed. We excluded theoretical papers, editorials, literature
reviews, and studies centred solely on higher education or corporate
training. Of the 112 unique records, 74 were eliminated at the title and
abstract stage because they did not address AI within a K–12
administrative or leadership context, lacked empirical grounding, or
were not published in eligible peer-reviewed outlets. The remaining 38
articles proceeded to full-text review. At that stage, 12 were excluded
for insufficient empirical data and 10 for concentrating on non-
administrative AI applications (e.g., purely pedagogical tools with no
school management dimension), yielding a final corpus of 16 studies.
All exclusion decisions were reached by consensus between the two
reviewers,     with       disagreements           resolved       through       structured
discussion.

To assess methodological quality, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool
(MMAT) Version 2018 was applied, which offers unified criteria for
qualitative, quantitative, and mixed‑methods designs. Two reviewers

The final corpus of 16 studies warrants contextual explanation. The
comparatively small number reflects the specificity of the inclusion
criteria: only empirical investigations addressing AI in K–12 school
management, rather than AI in education broadly, were eligible. This
domain is more recent and narrowly defined than general educational
technology research, and the quality threshold imposed by MMAT
further reduced the pool. The 16 included studies span nine countries
across four continents, encompass qualitative, quantitative, and
mixed-methods designs, and address a range of administrative
functions including scheduling, resource allocation, performance
monitoring, and predictive analytics. This diversity supports
meaningful cross-contextual synthesis even within a modest sample,
consistent with established practice in qualitative systematic reviews
where depth and interpretive rigour take precedence over volume
(Gough et al., 2017).

Data from the 16 included studies were extracted using a standardised
template capturing study objectives, methodological approaches, AI
tools employed, key findings (e.g., efficiency gains, equity impacts),
and reported challenges (e.g., infrastructural barriers, algorithmic
bias). A three-phase coding process in NVivo 15 was then conducted
to organise and interpret the extracted material. The figure of 2,143
data points refers to the total number of discrete meaning units,

individual sentences or clauses drawn from the included articles, that
were identified as relevant to AI in school management and subjected
to coding. Each unit was extracted verbatim, assigned to one or more
initial codes, and stored as a separate NVivo node, enabling
traceability from raw source text to final theme. During open coding,
two researchers independently identified 53 initial codes—terms such
as “automated scheduling,” “real-time analytics,” and “personalised
learning.” Disagreements, which constituted approximately 12% of
coding decisions, were reconciled through iterative discussion.

In the axial coding phase, these open codes were grouped into broader
categories reflecting functional domains of AI in school management,
such as administrative automation, data-driven decision-making, and
instructional support. NVivo facilitated the organisation of related
codes   into     hierarchical        nodes,       enabling           the   distillation   of
comprehensive concepts from granular observations. Finally, in the
selective coding phase, three overarching themes were synthesised
from    the    categories:       optimisation         of    managerial         workflows,
enhancement of personalised instruction, and integration of predictive
analytics for decision support. At each coding stage, we calculated
Cohen’s κ, 0.78 for open coding and 0.85 for axial coding, to verify
coding reliability. To manage researcher subjectivity throughout,
coders maintained a shared codebook that was updated iteratively as
new codes emerged, with any definitional changes recorded and
discussed before being applied retrospectively to previously coded
material. Peer debriefing sessions between the two coders were held at
each phase transition to surface and resolve interpretive assumptions
before they could compound across coding stages.

To support credibility and validity, methodological triangulation was
employed. Five external experts in educational technology and AI

This systematic review—guided by PRISMA, appraised via MMAT,
and analysed through NVivo—provides a methodologically rigorous
and transparent synthesis of recent empirical research on AI in K–12
administration. This four‑phase process, underpinned by stringent
inclusion criteria, dual‑reviewer screening, robust quality appraisal,
and iterative coding, ensures that the integrative framework is
grounded in high‑quality evidence. The resulting insights not only
map the current landscape of AI applications but also illuminate
systemic barriers and enablers, thereby offering actionable guidance
for policymakers, administrators, and researchers seeking sustainable,
equitable, and efficient AI integration in school settings.

                                     Discussion

The findings demonstrate that AI applications in K–12 school
management can substantially enhance administrative efficiency and
strategic decision-making, but also present organisational and ethical
complexities that demand robust theoretical framing and policy
interventions. Across the 16 reviewed studies, Enhancing Managerial
Efficiency emerged as the most prevalent theme, with 14 studies
reporting 20–40% reductions in scheduling and reporting time through
automated timetabling and resource allocation systems. However,
several investigations noted an initial “implementation dip,” wherein
customisation and staff training requirements temporarily increased
workloads—a phenomenon explained by Rogers’s Diffusion of
Innovations     theory,      which        emphasises          the   critical   role   of
organisational readiness and gradual adoption stages in realising
technology benefits (Sposato, 2025).

These findings also highlight that AI adoption is not simply a technical
upgrade but an organisational change process. When schools
introduced AI without simultaneously addressing staff confidence,
workflow redesign, or institutional culture, gains were slower and
resistance more pronounced. Lewin’s change management model,
which describes change as a process of unfreezing existing practices,
moving to new ones, and refreezing them as norms, offers a useful lens
here: schools that deliberately created space for experimentation,
reflection, and adjustment were more likely to achieve sustained

overreliance on algorithmic recommendations risks marginalising
educator expertise and contextual nuance, aligning with socio-
technical systems theory, which argues for a “human-in-the‑loop”
design to preserve professional agency and prevent techno-centric
governance. This concern connects directly to distributed leadership
scholarship (Spillane, 2006), which conceptualises leadership as a
practice distributed across roles and contexts rather than concentrated
in hierarchical authority. AI-driven decision-support tools, if designed
or deployed without adequate consultation, risk re-centralising
influence in the hands of those with technical access—or in the systems
themselves—at the expense of the collegial, context-sensitive decision-
making that distributed leadership models champion. Teacher
autonomy is a related pressure point: where AI systems prescribe
instructional    pathways          or     enable       fine-grained          performance
surveillance, educators may experience diminished professional
discretion rather than supported growth. School leaders therefore
carry a particular responsibility to frame AI as a tool that extends
rather than circumscribes professional judgment.

                                     Conclusion

This review synthesised evidence from 16 empirical studies to map
how AI is reshaping K–12 school management and to propose a
governance-oriented framework for sustainable, equitable adoption.
Six thematic functions emerged: (1) Managerial Efficiency, with 20–
40% reductions in administrative tasks when support structures were
in place; (2) Strategic Decision‑Making, where predictive analytics
dashboards      strengthened          leadership         choices;       (3)     Facilitating
Educational Processes, yielding 15–25% gains in student engagement
via adaptive platforms; (4) Resource Optimization & Security, through
intelligent budgeting and data‑protection systems; (5) Monitoring &
Evaluation, automating continuous performance assessments; and (6)
Transparency & Communication, enhancing stakeholder engagement
with real‑time reporting tools.

The study’s theoretical contribution lies in integrating the TOE
framework with distributed leadership and change management
theories to explain not only what AI does in schools but how it alters
the organisational conditions within which leadership is exercised.
This moves the conversation beyond technical functionality toward a
management science of AI adoption—one attentive to culture, power,
professional identity, and institutional capacity. Practically, the tiered
implementation framework and AI-ready leadership competency

adoption in ways that are sensitive to their school’s maturity level and
resource context. Taken together, these contributions offer a more
complete and actionable account of AI in school management than
prior reviews, which have tended to treat adoption as a technical rather
than organisational challenge.

The evidence also carries a clear message for the field of educational
leadership: the schools most likely to benefit from AI are those whose
leaders approach it as an organisational change process—one that
requires deliberate attention to professional culture, stakeholder trust,
ethical governance, and the preservation of human judgment at the
heart of educational decision-making. AI will not automatically
improve schools; its benefits depend on the quality of the leadership
and governance conditions within which it operates.
