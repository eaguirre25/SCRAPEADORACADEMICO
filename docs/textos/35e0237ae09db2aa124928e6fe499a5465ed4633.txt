INTRODUCTION
       Bullying in educational institutions has emerged as a pervasive global concern that significantly
undermines the fundamental principles of safe and conducive learning environments. Over the past
two decades, this phenomenon has increasingly attracted the attention of public stakeholders,
educators, and researchers who are concerned about its profound effects on school discipline,
institutional climate, student mental health, and academic achievement (Bosworth et al., 2018;
Espelage & Swearer, 2003; Farina, 2019). The phenomenon encompasses various forms of aggressive
behaviour, including physical attacks, verbal harassment, social exclusion, and psychological
intimidation, which collectively threaten the well-being and academic development of students across
all educational levels (Abdullah, 2023). Contemporary evidence reveals that bullying has evolved

             Journal of Innovation and Research in Primary Education | 4(4), 2025 | 1891-1903


beyond traditional face-to-face interactions to encompass cyberbullying, creating multifaceted
challenges that require comprehensive management approaches and whole-school interventions.
        The prevalence of bullying in Indonesian primary schools presents alarming statistics that
underscore the magnitude of this educational crisis. According to the KPAI report (2023), there were
3,172 documented cases of bullying in educational contexts during 2020-2023, with a consistent
upward trend annually. Furthermore, approximately 40% of primary school students in Indonesia
have reported experiencing various forms of bullying, encompassing physical, verbal, and social
manifestations (Utary, 2022). These statistics reveal not only the widespread nature of the problem
but also highlight the inadequacy of traditional packaged prevention programs, which research
indicates decrease bullying and victimization by only 17-23% on average (Fox et al., 2012).
        The detrimental impacts of bullying extend far beyond immediate physical or emotional harm,
creating cascading effects that influence victims, perpetrators, and witnesses alike. Children who
experience bullying frequently develop significant mental health complications, including depression,
anxiety disorders, and diminished academic motivation (Lusiana & Arifin, 2022). These negative
consequences can persist into long-term trajectories involving substance abuse, delinquency, social
isolation, and academic failure (Kaiser & Rasminsky, 2003). Research conducted by Borualogo et al.
(2022) demonstrates that bullying victims exhibit substantially higher risks of developing psychological
disorders compared to their non-bullied peers. Conversely, children who engage in bullying behaviour
tend to develop antisocial tendencies that persist into adulthood (Nenotek et al., 2025), while
bystanders often experience guilt and helplessness that adversely affects their psychological
development (Auliffe et al., 2022).
        Contemporary research has increasingly emphasized the critical role of school climate and
comprehensive management approaches in bullying prevention. Thapa and colleagues (2012) define
school climate as the quality and character of school life encompassing both psychosocial factors such
as positive relationships and connectedness, and organizational factors including policies and
administrative commitment. Safe, caring, participatory, and responsive school climates foster
attachment and bonding to school, thereby reducing prevalence of victimization and aggression
(Gregory et al., 2010). Wang, Berry, and Swearer (2013) demonstrated that when school climate is
negative, improving the institutional climate becomes a crucial strategy for preventing bullying
behaviours. Research indicates that systematic school climate approaches must integrate preventive
interventions with reactive responses to bullying incidents (Ansary et al., 2015).
        The implementation of effective school management systems has proven crucial for successful
bullying prevention programs. Wilson (2004) found that implementation level was the most important
variable in determining intervention effectiveness, while Durlak and DuPre (2008) established that
positive results occur when implementation levels reach at least 60%. Leadership engagement has
been identified as a critical factor in successful implementation, with Bryk (2010) demonstrating that
principals can produce institutional change by cultivating cadres of leaders including teachers, parents,
and community members. Furthermore, organizational capacity and readiness for change, defined as
the necessary motivation and ability to implement and sustain effective interventions, depend heavily
on leadership commitment and active engagement (Durlak & DuPre, 2008).
        Contemporary research contributions have examined various aspects of school management in
bullying prevention. Bosworth et al. (2018) conducted an exploratory study examining leadership
involvement in enhancing school climate and reducing bullying in high school contexts. Halizah et al.
(2024) analyzed the role of multicultural education management in reducing discrimination and
bullying in primary schools. Hidayat et al. (2023) investigated school management implementation in
preventing both traditional and cyberbullying. Karim et al. (2023) examined educational foundations
in optimizing teacher roles and school management for bullying prevention. Maryono et al. (2024)
explored guidance and counselling management approaches, while Novriyansah et al. (2024)
investigated counselling management for bullying anticipation. Sabrina et al. (2025) analyzed student

management strategies, and Victorynie (2017) examined effective classroom management approaches
in primary schools.
       Despite these valuable contributions, significant gaps remain in understanding contextually
responsive school management approaches to bullying prevention. Most existing studies have focused
on single institutional contexts or specific management aspects, lacking comprehensive comparative
analyses that consider environmental, socio-economic, and cultural factors influencing management
effectiveness. Limited research has explored the integration of local wisdom and community resources
in school management strategies, particularly in resource-constrained rural settings where social
capital and traditional values may serve as powerful prevention mechanisms.
       The novelty of this research lies in its comprehensive comparative approach to analyzing school
management implementation across different geographical and socio-economic contexts. Unlike
previous studies examining management functions in isolation, this research employs Deming's (1986)
four-stage management cycle as a comprehensive framework for understanding how contextual
factors influence management effectiveness. This study addresses the critical need for adaptive
management models that function effectively in both rural and urban primary school environments
while considering resource limitations and community characteristics.
       This research aims to analyze differences in bullying prevention management between primary
schools in rural and urban areas, examine contextual factors influencing management effectiveness,
and formulate adaptive management models suitable for both contexts. The study seeks to develop
strategies utilizing local wisdom and community resources while providing evidence-based policy
recommendations for educational stakeholders. The significance extends beyond immediate
institutional benefits, contributing to contextually responsive educational management understanding
and providing practical frameworks for improving student safety across diverse educational settings.

METHODS
       This study employed a qualitative research design utilizing a comparative case study approach
to examine school management practices in bullying prevention across different geographical and
socio-economic contexts. The qualitative methodology was selected to facilitate in-depth exploration
of complex phenomena including institutional behaviors, management strategies, and contextual
factors that influence bullying prevention effectiveness, as recommended by Marshall and Rossman
(2014). This approach enabled comprehensive investigation of the meanings, actions, and attitudes
underlying school management practices while allowing for detailed examination of how contextual
differences between rural and urban settings influence implementation strategies (Cohen et al., 2017).
       The research was conducted at two purposively selected primary schools in Cianjur Regency,
West Java, Indonesia, chosen based on their contrasting geographical locations and documented
history of implementing bullying prevention programs. SD Negeri Kebonpeuteuy 2, located in
Gekbrong Sub-district, represented the rural context, while SD Negeri Sayang 2 in Cianjur Sub-district
served as the urban representative. These institutions were selected through criterion sampling to
ensure they possessed sufficient experience with bullying prevention initiatives and could provide rich
data regarding management implementation across different environmental contexts. The study
participants comprised school principals, vice principals, guidance counselors, classroom teachers, and
selected administrative staff who were directly involved in bullying prevention management, totaling
twelve key informants across both institutions.
       Data collection was conducted through triangulated methods to ensure comprehensive
understanding and validation of findings. Systematic observation served as the primary data collection
method, employing structured observation protocols to document daily interactions, behavioral
patterns, institutional climate, and management practices during regular school activities. Particular
attention was paid to vulnerable periods such as break times, transition periods, and extracurricular
activities when bullying incidents typically occur. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with key
informants to gather detailed insights regarding management strategies, implementation challenges,

             Journal of Innovation and Research in Primary Education | 4(4), 2025 | 1891-1903


and contextual factors influencing program effectiveness. Interview protocols were designed with
open-ended questions that allowed participants to elaborate on their experiences while maintaining
focus on the research objectives. Document analysis constituted the third data collection method,
involving systematic examination of institutional policies, anti-bullying procedures, incident reports,
meeting minutes, program evaluation documents, and correspondence related to bullying prevention
efforts.
       The data collection process followed a systematic sequence beginning with document analysis
to establish baseline understanding of institutional policies and procedures, followed by in-depth
interviews with key stakeholders to gather perspectives on management implementation, and
concluding with observational activities to witness actual practice implementation. This sequential
approach facilitated progressive data verification and enabled researchers to clarify discrepancies
between documented policies and actual practices during subsequent interviews and observations.
Data saturation was achieved when no new themes or patterns emerged from additional data
collection activities.
       Data analysis employed thematic content analysis utilizing an inductive approach to identify
patterns and themes emerging from the collected data. Interview recordings were transcribed
verbatim and systematically coded using both descriptive and interpretive codes. The coding process
involved multiple iterations of data examination, with initial codes refined and consolidated into
broader thematic categories that reflected the four management functions outlined in Deming's cycle:
planning, implementation, monitoring, and follow-up. Cross-case analysis was conducted to identify
similarities and differences between rural and urban contexts, while within-case analysis examined the
coherence and effectiveness of management practices within each institution. Data triangulation was
employed to enhance validity by comparing findings across different data sources and identifying
convergent themes. Member checking was conducted with key participants to verify the accuracy of
interpretations and ensure that findings authentically represented their experiences and perspectives,
thereby enhancing the credibility and trustworthiness of the research conclusions.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Results
Planning
        The results showed that the planning of the bullying prevention programme at SD Negeri
Kebonpeuteuy 2 is still simple and adaptive to the local context. Based on interviews with principals
and teachers, it is known that planning is carried out through teacher meetings at the beginning of
the school year. Some of the components discussed include: problem identification, goal setting,
programme preparation, allocation of funding sources. Overall, planning at SD Negeri Kebonpeuteuy 2
prioritises a family atmosphere and traditional values as a basis for prevention.
        At SD Negeri sayang 2, school management planning in preventing bullying is systematic and
documented. The interview results show that the planning stages include: situation analysis, policy
formulation, comprehensive programme preparation, budgeting, scheduling, setting success
indicators. The communication strategy to all school members is structured through various channels.
This approach reflects the principle of stakeholder involvement in education policy implementation
(Suharti, 2021).
        Based on the findings of interviews and observations at SDN Kebonpeuteuy 2, the planning
process in school management to prevent bullying is generally simple. In the new school year, the
principal and teachers discussed the issue of bullying in a meeting. However, the principal stated that
the results of the meeting are not yet fully based on empirical data that accurately represents the
patterns of bullying that occur in the school. Although operational standards for handling cases have
been developed, the implementation of the programme often experiences obstacles, especially due to
the limited knowledge and skills of teachers in identifying and handling bullying cases. To build
collective awareness, the school disseminates the anti-bullying programme at the beginning of the

school year to all school members, including the committee and parents. However, active participation
from all stakeholders still needs to be improved to make the planning process more inclusive, adaptive
and participatory.

Organising
        The implementation of the bullying prevention programme at SD Negeri Kebonpeuteuy 2
focuses more on local culture-based and informal approaches. Based on the results of interviews with
principals and teachers, it was identified that the implementation of activities was carried out flexibly,
adjusting to the dynamics of students' daily lives and the school environment.
        Some of the initiatives taken include: Integration of local wisdom where local cultural values of
silih asah silih asih and silih asuh are integrated in the learning process to shape student character to
be able to appreciate and care for others (Rahmah, 2020). At SD Negeri Kebonpeuteuy 2, it has been
running every morning from 07.15 to 07.30 different habituation is carried out where on Monday the
flag ceremony, Tuesday singing national songs, Wednesday nyunda where material containing
Sundanese elements is given, on Thursday the dhuha prayer is held together, Friday the cultum is
held and on Saturday sports together. Before habituation activities are always given a briefing on the
dangers of bullying and what to do and not to do is always reminded every day.
        At SD Negeri Sayang 2, the implementation of the bullying prevention programme is different
from SD Kebonpeuteuy 2 where it is implemented systematically and based on formal policies.
Interviews with school management showed that the approach used was comprehensive and
structured. Some of the programmes implemented are: The first programme is the "School Without
Bullying" campaign, this activity is carried out regularly and involves all elements of the school
through various creative activities, such as poster competitions and joint commitment declarations.
The second programme is integration in the curriculum, bullying prevention material is included in
Pancasila and Religious Education lessons. The third programme is the peer programme, where
students are trained to detect, report and assist their friends who are potentially being bullied.
        In terms of organisation, SDN Kebonpeuteuy 2 has formed a special team to handle bullying
prevention and handling efforts, which consists of the counselling guidance teacher, homeroom
teacher, and a number of appointed teachers. However, according to the deputy principal for student
affairs, the structure of the division of roles in the team has not been running optimally and
systematically. Some important functions such as case recording, assistance for victims, and
rehabilitation for perpetrators are still managed manually, with inadequate documentation. Another
obstacle is the limited number of educators who have special expertise in handling bullying, as well as
the double workload because they also have to carry out teaching duties.
        Bullying prevention management efforts in this school are implemented with an approach that
is tailored to the school's capabilities and resources. Prevention programmes usually come from
internal initiatives, supported by policies from the local education office. These activities include social
skills training, anti-bullying campaigns and active involvement of teachers and students. Schools are
also slowly building an inclusive culture through the implementation of co-operative learning and the
reinforcement of the values of tolerance and mutual respect.
Surveillance
       Supervision of student behaviour at SD Negeri Kebonpeuteuy 2 is direct, based on close
personal relationships. Based on interviews with the principal and teachers, the approach used reflects
a family feel and active community involvement. Some of the supervision mechanisms applied include:
direct observation, where class teachers and picket teachers actively observe student behaviour
during school activities, especially at vulnerable times such as recess and during sports activities, this
method is commonly used in small schools that rely on social closeness between teachers and
students. The school also communicates with parents either directly or by using wa media or phone
calls to monitor student behaviour outside the school environment. Case recording is still done
conventionally by writing in manual notebooks managed by each class teacher. A structured data

analysis system is not yet in place, and evaluation of the bullying prevention programme is conducted
informally in a teachers' council meeting held at the end of each semester.
       At SD Negeri Sayang 2, the monitoring system against bullying is modern, systematic and
technology-based. Interviews with school management showed that a structured system has been
implemented to facilitate case detection and handling. Some of the supervision strategies
implemented include: a multilevel reporting system, which means that the school forms a reporting
line starting with reporting to the homeroom teacher, the homeroom teacher continues the report to
the counselling teacher, and finally it is reported to the child-friendly school team.
       The second strategy is the anonymous complaint box, where anonymous suggestion or
complaint boxes are placed at strategic points in the school environment and opened regularly by the
management team to follow up on reports. The third strategy is the school climate survey, which is
conducted by the school periodically to assess the safety, comfort, and social dynamics in the school.
The results are used as the basis for evaluating the violence prevention programme. Furthermore, the
school also provides a web-based reporting system that can be accessed by students and parents, as
part of the effort to digitalise the case management system.
       All case data is collected and analysed using the school management information system.
Evaluations are conducted periodically by the child-friendly school team to look at bullying patterns
and adjust ongoing prevention programmes.
       The supervision process is conducted regularly by the principal and the bullying prevention
team. This includes daily monitoring of interactions between students and systematic monthly
evaluations. The case notebook is the main tool in documenting case data, including the frequency of
occurrence, bullying patterns, and handling steps taken. In addition, the school also provides an
anonymous reporting mechanism and encourages the participation of teachers, students and parents
as part of early detection of potential bullying.
       Despite these efforts, SDN Kebonpeuteuy 2 still faces complex challenges. The principal said
that the school community's understanding of the types of bullying and how to handle it is still limited.
The process of recording and handling cases has not utilised a digital system, making it seem manual
and inefficient. The lack of a dedicated budget for prevention programmes means that the school
relies heavily on internal initiatives and creativity, which unfortunately is not enough to overcome the
complexity of the bullying problem. One pressing issue is the unavailability of professionals such as
psychologists who can provide intensive assistance to victims and perpetrators of bullying.

Follow-up
        The handling of bullying cases at SD Negeri Kebonpeuteuy 2 was carried out in stages by
prioritising a family approach and local culture. Based on the results of the interviews, the school
implemented a number of strategies as a form of response to bullying incidents. The steps taken
include: dialogical mediation, for mild cases, the class teacher facilitates a meeting between the
perpetrator and the victim in an open dialogue atmosphere to build mutual understanding and
agreement. The school also conducts individual counselling where the perpetrator and victim are
given personal counselling to explore the root of the problem and design long-term solutions using
psychopedagogical principles in elementary schools.
        The school also conducts meetings with parents where the school invites the parents of the
students involved to discuss with the school in handling the case and finding the best solution. For the
perpetrators of bullying, the school applies educational sanctions, the punishment is given so that the
perpetrators realise their mistakes and do not repeat them in the future. The evaluation conducted by
SD Negeri Kebonpeuteuy 2 is informally in a teacher meeting at the end of the semester, with a family
approach based on the teacher's experience in handling cases. There are no definite evaluation
indicators.
        Follow-up to bullying cases at SD Negeri Sayang 2 is carried out through formal and systematic
procedures. Interviews with the school management team showed that the school has a
comprehensive and documented mechanism. Some of the strategies used include: initial assessment

where the school team conducts an assessment to assess the seriousness of the case and determine
the appropriate form of intervention to be taken by the school. At SD Negeri sayang 2, bullying cases
are handled in stages, ranging from mild reprimands to administrative actions such as school transfers
for severe cases. To overcome complex cases, the school involves an external psychologist as part of
intersectoral collaboration in education.
       Programme evaluation at SD Negeri Sayang 2 is conducted systematically using measurable
indicators such as frequency of cases, speed of response, and students' perceptions of school safety.
The data becomes the basis for improving programme policies for the following year.
       As a form of adaptation to these obstacles, the school has taken a strategic step by forming an
"Anti-Bullying Ambassador" from among the students. These ambassadors play an active role in
detecting and reporting potential bullying in the school environment. In addition, internal training for
teachers is also continuously sought to strengthen their capacity in managing bullying cases, given the
limited access to external training. The school has also developed a safer reporting system and
increased the role of parents in monitoring children's behaviour, as part of a collaborative prevention
strengthening strategy.

Discussion
       The findings of this study reveal significant variations in school management approaches to
bullying prevention, reflecting the profound influence of contextual factors on implementation
effectiveness. These results align with previous research emphasizing the importance of adaptive
management strategies while highlighting unique challenges and opportunities present in different
educational environments.
       The planning phase differences between rural and urban schools can be analyzed through
Deming's (1986) management theory, which emphasizes the foundational importance of systematic
planning for effective implementation. The rural school's emphasis on cultural integration and informal
planning processes demonstrates what Thapa et al. (2012) identified as the psychosocial dimension of
school climate, where cultural values and community relationships form the foundation of institutional
approaches. This finding aligns with Coleman and Hoffer's (1987) functional community theory, which
suggests that schools embedded in value communities with shared beliefs and expectations can
leverage social capital for effective interventions.
       The integration of local Sundanese values (silih asah, silih asih, silih asuh) in rural planning
exemplifies what Ansary et al. (2015) described as holistic theoretical approaches based on social
ecological theory. This cultural integration supports Halizah et al.'s (2024) findings regarding
multicultural education management, but extends beyond their work by demonstrating how
indigenous wisdom can serve as organizational capacity for change. Durlak and DuPre's (2008)
organizational readiness framework suggests that such cultural alignment may enhance the
"necessary motivation and ability to identify, select, plan, implement, evaluate, and sustain effective
interventions."
       Conversely, the urban school's systematic, documentation-heavy approach reflects what Weiner
(2009) termed organizational readiness through formal structures and processes. This approach aligns
with Bryk's (2010) research on formal leadership as a driver of change, where principals cultivate
systematic approaches to stakeholder engagement. The comprehensive policy frameworks observed
in the urban setting demonstrate what Durlak and DuPre (2008) identified as critical factors for
implementation success: clear priorities and structured change management processes. However, this
formal approach may lack the community embeddedness that Coleman and Hoffer (1987) identified as
crucial for sustainable educational interventions.
       Implementation strategies reveal how contextual factors shape prevention approaches within
broader theoretical frameworks of school climate intervention. Wang, Berry, and Swearer's (2013)
assertion that improving school climate is crucial for preventing bullying behaviors is evidenced
differently in each context. The rural school's morning habituation programs demonstrate what



                                                  1897

             Journal of Innovation and Research in Primary Education | 4(4), 2025 | 1891-1903


Gregory et al. (2010) described as authoritative school climate practices that foster attachment and
bonding to reduce victimization and aggression.
        The rural implementation approach reflects Low and Van Ryzin's (2014) findings regarding
positive psychosocial climate as foundational to bullying prevention. The daily integration of cultural
activities, religious practices, and community values creates what Thapa et al. (2012) defined as the
"quality and character of school life" that encompasses both psychosocial and organizational factors.
This approach supports Espelage, Polanin, and Low's (2014) research demonstrating that staff
commitment and positive relationships are associated with reduced bullying, fighting, and peer
victimization.
        The urban school's formal peer ambassador program and systematic campaigns reflect what
Ertesvag (2015) identified as whole-school interventions involving all students, staff, and parents. The
"School Without Bullying" campaign exemplifies Bradshaw et al.'s (2009) integrated approach
combining specific prevention programs with whole-school climate interventions. This systematic
approach aligns with Swain-Bradway et al.'s (2015) recommendations for team leadership and
systematic assessment in bullying prevention efforts.
        However, the urban approach also demonstrates potential limitations identified in Fox,
Farrington, and Ttofi's (2012) meta-analysis, where packaged prevention programs showed only
moderate effectiveness (17-23% reduction). The formal structure, while comprehensive, may lack the
organic community integration that Coleman and Hoffer (1987) suggested provides sustainable
behavioral change through social network density and value consistency.
        Monitoring system differences highlight how organizational capacity manifests differently across
contexts, reflecting Wilson's (2004) finding that implementation level determines intervention
effectiveness. The rural school's reliance on personal relationships and direct observation
demonstrates what De Nobile et al. (2016) identified as essential leadership practices for whole-school
behavior management systems, though through informal rather than systematic mechanisms.
        The rural approach reflects Hauge et al.'s (2014) collective and distributive leadership model,
where capacity building occurs through community engagement rather than formal structures. This
aligns with Durlak and DuPre's (2008) organizational capacity framework, but suggests that capacity
can be built through social capital rather than solely through formal systems. The reliance on
WhatsApp communication and informal parent engagement demonstrates adaptation to local
communication patterns while maintaining the stakeholder involvement that Bosworth et al. (2018)
identified as crucial for climate enhancement.
        The urban school's sophisticated digital monitoring systems exemplify Park and Datnow's
(2009) research on district leadership providing infrastructure and resources for ongoing professional
learning. The anonymous reporting systems and web-based platforms reflect what Wanless and
Domitrovich (2015) described as shared vision and priorities supported by technological infrastructure.
This approach demonstrates Durlak and DuPre's (2008) systematic implementation requirements
reaching the 60% threshold necessary for positive outcomes.
        However, the technological sophistication may create what Thaker et al. (2008) identified as
implementation barriers when leadership support or user engagement is insufficient. The digital divide
between rural and urban contexts reflects broader inequalities that Borualogo et al. (2022)
documented regarding infrastructure disparities, suggesting that monitoring effectiveness depends on
contextual appropriateness rather than technological sophistication alone.
        Follow-up strategies reveal fundamental differences in conflict resolution philosophies that
reflect broader theoretical orientations toward behavior change and community healing. The rural
school's emphasis on dialogical mediation demonstrates what Haris et al. (2024) described as
restorative justice approaches focusing on relationship restoration rather than punitive sanctions. This
approach aligns with Ryan and Smith's (2009) "socially complex services" integration, where
interventions address underlying social dynamics rather than isolated behaviors.

       The rural approach reflects Cornell and Bradshaw's (2015) research on changing school climate
to support positive relationships and decrease harassment through comprehensive environmental
modification. The family-oriented intervention strategies demonstrate what Coleman and Hoffer
(1987) described as functional community approaches where school-family-community connections
create sustainable behavioral change mechanisms.
       The urban school's formal procedures and external professional support demonstrate systematic
implementation of Ross and Horner's (2009) tiered intervention approach within SW-PBIS frameworks.
The involvement of external psychologists reflects Ansary et al.'s (2015) recommendations for
indicated-level interventions addressing severe cases through specialized professional support. This
approach demonstrates what Pugh and Chitiyo (2012) described as integrated specific prevention
programs with whole-school climate interventions.
       However, the formal approach may lack the community embeddedness that Espelage, Polanin,
and Low (2014) identified as crucial for sustainable change. The emphasis on administrative
consequences rather than relationship restoration may not address the social ecological factors that
Ansary et al. (2015) identified as fundamental to effective prevention.
       The comparative findings suggest that effective bullying prevention management requires
integration of formal systematic approaches with community-embedded cultural strategies. Coleman
and Hoffer's (1987) functional community theory provides insight into rural effectiveness through
social capital leverage, while Durlak and DuPre's (2008) organizational capacity framework explains
urban systematic advantages. Neither approach alone optimizes prevention effectiveness, suggesting
need for hybrid models that combine systematic implementation with cultural responsiveness.
       The findings extend Weiner's (2009) organizational readiness theory by demonstrating that
readiness can be built through cultural alignment and community engagement rather than solely
through formal capacity building. This supports Hidayat's (2024) emphasis on mobilizing cooperation
between sectors while building on local strengths and resources. The results suggest that future
bullying prevention management should integrate systematic evaluation and documentation with
culturally responsive implementation strategies, creating adaptive frameworks that leverage both
formal organizational capacity and community social capital for sustainable intervention effectiveness.

CONCLUSION
       This comparative study reveals that effective school management in bullying prevention
requires contextually responsive approaches that integrate systematic implementation with
community-embedded cultural strategies. The research demonstrates significant variations between
rural and urban primary schools across all four management functions of Deming's cycle. Rural
schools leverage cultural capital through informal, relationship-based approaches emphasizing local
wisdom integration, while urban schools employ systematic, technology-enhanced strategies with
formal documentation and professional support networks. Both approaches demonstrate effectiveness
within their respective contexts, suggesting that successful prevention management depends on
adaptive implementation rather than standardized procedures.
       The study contributes to educational management literature by extending organizational
readiness theory to demonstrate that capacity can be built through cultural alignment and community
engagement rather than solely through formal structures. It provides empirical evidence for hybrid
management models that combine systematic evaluation with culturally responsive implementation,
addressing gaps in comparative analyses of context-specific bullying prevention strategies. The
research advances understanding of how social ecological factors influence management effectiveness
and offers practical frameworks for developing adaptive prevention policies.
       Practical implications include the need for education policymakers to develop flexible guidelines
that accommodate diverse institutional contexts while maintaining evidence-based standards. School
administrators should consider integrating cultural resources with systematic monitoring systems,
particularly in resource-constrained environments. Teacher preparation programs should emphasize



                                                  1899

culturally responsive management strategies alongside formal intervention techniques. The findings
suggest that effective prevention requires sustained community engagement and stakeholder
collaboration rather than top-down policy implementation.
       Research limitations include the focus on two institutions within a single geographic region,
which may limit transferability to other cultural contexts. The qualitative design, while providing rich
contextual understanding, limits statistical generalizability of findings. Future research should expand
to multiple regions and cultural contexts, incorporate longitudinal designs to assess intervention
sustainability, and develop quantitative measures for evaluating hybrid management model
effectiveness. Mixed-methods studies examining the integration of formal and informal approaches
across diverse educational settings would enhance understanding of optimal prevention management
