INTRODUCTION
       Education is a public service that combines the fulfillment of rights, professional processes, and long-
term results. Residents experience their quality through school access, student admission, information,
learning, protection, services for disabilities, assistance, communication, complaints, and learning
outcomes. Therefore, quality is not enough to assess the number of schools, activities, or documents. The
assessment must see whether students obtain a process that is safe, inclusive, relevant, sustainable, and able
to develop competencies and character.
       Education policies provide objectives, rules, standards, programs, resources, and the division of
authority. But formal decisions do not result in change by themselves. Implementation takes place through
interlevel communication, officer interpretation, budget and manpower availability, cross-organizational
coordination, procedures, data, oversight, and target group responses. Gaps between design and practice can
arise when goals are unclear, rules change without support, resources are not appropriate, or schools face
conditions that policymakers did not consider.
       School management works at the level closest to the learners. Principals, teachers, education staff,
committees, and partners change policies into schedules, learning, task sharing, budget utilization, student
services, facilities management, and family relationships. Schools need autonomy to adjust ways, but remain

whether government support becomes a tangible improvement or stops as an undirected resource.
        Cianjur Regency has a large area with a variety of urban, rural, mountainous, and coastal
characteristics. The publication of Cianjur Regency in Figures 2025 provides an overview of geography,
government, demography, social, economic, and education as the basis for planning (BPS Cianjur Regency,
2025). Variations in the area make the cost of access, distribution of teachers, connectivity, transportation,
maintenance of facilities, and family support different. Administratively uniform policies can produce non-
uniform impacts if differences in capacity are not recognized.
        Cianjur also has a disaster risk context. The 2022 earthquake affected facilities and service continuity
in some areas, while the threat of earthquakes, landslides, floods, and fires demanded continuous
preparedness. The Cianjur Regency BPBD in 2025 will carry out socialization of disaster safety education
through presentations, simulations, and demonstrations. In 2026, post-earthquake school revitalization will
still be part of strengthening certain educational facilities. This fact shows that the quality of service includes
safety, infrastructure resilience, continuity plans, psychosocial support, and the ability to restore learning.
        Indonesia's legal framework regulates educational goals, national standards, regional authorities,
public services, and Minimum Service Standards. Law Number 20 of 2003 regulates the National Education
System. Law Number 23 of 2014 divides education affairs between levels of government. Government
Regulation Number 57 of 2021 as amended by Government Regulation Number 4 of 2022 establishes
National Education Standards. Permendikbudristek Number 32 of 2022 regulates technical standards for
minimum education services. The framework requires access as well as the quality of learning and the
educational environment.
        The Education Report Cards strengthen data-driven planning through the dimensions of learning
outcomes, equity, educator competence, learning quality, school climate, and education unit management.
The cycle of identification, reflection, and improvement helps local governments and schools choose
priorities based on the root of the problem. However, national data needs to be combined with local
information such as distance, building conditions, attendance, population displacement, disaster risk,
complaints, and family experiences. The platform does not replace analysis and dialogue at the regional
level.
        Local policies show that implementation requires operational interpretation. The 2025 Cianjur
Regency Disdikpora Circular Letter regarding effective learning hours, for example, regulates the start time
and duration of learning at various levels and provides space for determining certain patterns by the
authorities. Its implementation does not only depend on the text, but also on transportation readiness, travel
safety, family routines, teachers' conditions, facilities, and supervision. This example shows the need for
impact evaluation and feedback mechanisms.
        Previous research has shown that improving school management in low- and middle-income countries
can influence processes and outcomes, but the impact depends on program design, capacity, and context.
The school-based management literature also emphasizes a combination of autonomy, participation,
leadership, accountability, and support. Policy implementation research emphasizes the role of goals,
resources, disposition, communication, and structure. A synthesis is still needed that links the quality of
government-level implementation with school management capacity and service quality as a shared
outcome.
        This study answers three questions. First, how does the implementation of education policies affect
the quality of services in Cianjur Regency? Second, how does school management translate policies into
services? Third, what conditions strengthen or hinder these relationships? The goal is to develop conceptual
models, evaluation indicators, and recommendations that are relevant to the district government, education
offices, supervisors, schools, committees, and the community.


METHOD
      The study uses an explanatory qualitative approach with an integrative literature review design and
document analysis. The term influence is understood as a relationship mechanism based on theory and
consistency of evidence, not statistical coefficients. This design was chosen because there is no primary

implementation, school management functions, service quality indicators, the context of Cianjur Regency,
and factors that mediate or moderate relationships.
       The data sources consist of laws and regulations, publications of the Cianjur Regency BPS, official
documents of local governments, BPBD information, publications of the Ministry of Education and
Education, the framework of the Education Report Card, as well as scientific articles on policy
implementation, school-based management, leadership, quality assurance, educational resilience, and
service quality. Sources are selected based on relevance, publisher identity, traceability, and their ability to
provide norms, concepts, contexts, indicators, or evidence of implementation.
       Collection was carried out through search for terms in Indonesian and English, filtering by focus,
document reading, and matrix recording. Each source was recorded according to policy objectives, actors,
resources, processes, school level, outcomes, risks, and implications. Local information is only used when
sourced from an official or verifiable institution. The article does not claim interviews, surveys,
observations, or statistical results that were not conducted.
       The analysis takes place in four stages. First, open coding to find themes. Second, grouping themes
into policy quality, implementation process, school management capacity, service quality, and context.
Third, comparison between sources to find suitability and differences. Fourth, the preparation of a cause-
and-effect chain from policy to action, changes in school capacity, service processes, and student outcomes.
Credibility is maintained through triangulation of regulations, data, local policies, and literature.
       The limitations of the study are the inability to determine the magnitude of the influence, variations
between sub-districts, or certain school conditions. Conclusions are analytical synthesis that need to be
tested. Follow-up research can use multilevel surveys, longitudinal administrative data, interviews,
observations, facility audits, and educational report card results. Comparisons before and after policies or
between regions with different levels of support can strengthen causal inferences.


RESULTS
Dimensions of Education Policy Implementation
        The results of the synthesis identify seven dimensions of implementation. First, clarity of objectives
and consistency of standards. Implementers need to understand the problem to be solved, target groups,
outcomes, limits of authority, and priorities. Goals that are too general result in different interpretations,
while targets that are only activities encourage administrative compliance. Policies need to connect inputs,
activities, outputs, and service results. Changes must be accompanied by explanations, transition periods,
and support.
        Second, cross-level communication. Policies move from the central government, provinces, districts,
offices, supervisors, principals, teachers, students, and families. Any move can change meaning. Effective
communication provides official scripts, operational summaries, case examples, question channels,
controlled versions, and feedback. One-way socialization is not enough. Implementers need to be able to
convey difficulties, while policymakers need to explain or improve provisions that cannot be implemented.
        Third, the adequacy and appropriateness of resources. Budget, teachers, education personnel, time,
data, devices, facilities, transportation, connectivity, and competencies determine the ability of schools.
Adequacy does not only mean quantity, but distribution and suitability. Schools with disaster risk or difficult
geographical access require different forms of support. Policies that impose obligations without resources
shift the burden to schools and families, and widen inequality.
        Fourth, the disposition and capacity of the implementer. Principals and teachers need to understand
the purpose, believe in the benefits, have the skills, and gain support. Resistance does not necessarily
indicate a rejection of change; it can be a signal of unclear rules, overload, value conflicts, or safety risks.
Involvement of implementers in trials, task-based training, coaching, community of practice, and technical
assistance increases capability and ownership.
        Fifth, structure and coordination. Education implementation involves office, planning, finance,
personnel, public works, social, health, population, BPBD, sub-districts, villages, and schools. The problem
of children not going to school, damaged facilities, disabilities, or disaster recovery is not solved by one

School Management Dimensions
       Data-driven planning is the first dimension. Schools combine Education Report Cards, student data,
attendance, assessments, facility conditions, complaints, disaster risk, and input from school residents. The
process of identification, reflection, and improvement helps to identify the root of the problem. The plan
needs to establish actions, those in charge, schedules, resources, indicators, and review forums. A long list
of programs without priorities makes energy scattered and difficult to evaluate.
       Leadership is the link between policy and practice. Principals provide direction, build a safe and
learning culture, protect learning time, develop teachers, and manage change. Leadership doesn't stop at
administration. Leaders need to explain reasons, hear concerns, break down barriers, and adjust support.
Supervisors and agencies help through coaching and resources, not just document checks.
       The management of teachers and education personnel determines the continuity of services.
Assignments, burdens, attendance, competencies, collaboration, well-being, and professional development
affect the quality of the classroom. Training is directed to the needs of learning outcomes and the context of
students, then assessed through implementation. Teacher distribution needs to look at subjects, number of
study groups, location, and special needs. Schools develop teams so that services do not depend on one
person.
       Curriculum and learning management translates standards into student experience. Schools set
calendars, effective hours, goals, diagnostic assessments, differentiation, remedial, enrichment, and support
strategies. Time requirements should not only be met on schedules; schools need to maintain actual learning
time and student readiness. Outcome analysis is used to improve teaching, not just reporting or ranking.
       Transparent and priority-based financing allows policies to be implemented. Work plans and budgets
need to be linked to diagnosis, not spending habits. Committee participation and easy-to-understand

information strengthen accountability. Procurement takes into account time, quality, maintenance, and life-
cycle costs. In at-risk areas, budgets also consider preparedness, backup, and service recovery.
       Facilities, infrastructure, and technology are assessed according to function, safety, accessibility, and
resilience. The existence of space is insufficient if it is damaged, unsafe, not inclusive, or does not support
learning. Schools need an inventory of conditions, risk priorities, maintenance schedules, and damage
reporting pathways. Post-earthquake revitalization needs to connect safe construction with learning spaces,
practical needs, evacuation, and sustainability of use.
       Student services include admission, orientation, guidance, health, protection, assistance,
developmental activities, transfers, and complaints. Procedures should be child-friendly, maintain
confidentiality, and establish referrals. Vulnerable groups require accommodations based on needs.
Communication with families needs to be consistent and provide space for dialogue. Discipline should not
disregard rights, dignity, or causes of behavior.
       Partnerships and quality assurance close the cycle. Committees, parents, villages, health services,
social services, BPBD, the business world, and the community can provide information and support.
Partnerships have goals, roles, student protection, and evaluation. Quality assurance compares plans,
implementations, processes, results, and experiences. Findings generate improvements and are shared so
that organizations learn.
Influence on the Quality of Education Services
       Policy implementation affects quality through system clarity and support, whereas school
management affects quality through the quality of support use. The relationship is complementary. Good
but poorly managed policies do not reach students. Management is strong but policies are inconsistent with
limited resources and uncertainty. Alignment occurs when government goals are translated into school
priorities, resources follow needs, and school data influences policy improvement.
       The quality of service in this study includes access, reliability, responsiveness, certainty, inclusivity,
security, relevance, learning outcomes, satisfaction, and trust. Access assesses ease of obtaining services;
reliability assesses consistency; responsiveness assesses assistance; certainty assesses procedures and
timing; inclusivity assesses the fulfillment of different needs; safety assesses physical and psychological
protection; relevance and outcomes assess core benefits; satisfaction and trust capture the overall experience.
       School capacity mediates relationships. New policy resources produce benefits when schools can
plan, organize, implement, and evaluate. In contrast, the quality of implementation moderates management
impact. Competent principals struggle when data, budgets, teachers, or facilities are not available. This
model explains why evaluation needs to measure both levels and avoid the habit of blaming educational
units for systemic problems.
       The context of Cianjur is an important moderator. Region, connectivity, transportation, disaster risk,
building conditions, socioeconomic status, and family capacity affect access and response. Equity requires
proportional support, not identical inputs. Data should be sorted by sub-district, level, school status,
disability, and social group. The average score of the district can cover disparities and different recovery
times.

Implementation and Control Risks
       The first risk is compliance-oriented policies. Schools can prioritize documents, activity photos, and
system entries over learning changes. Control is done by reducing reports, using data already available, and
assessing evidence of processes and outcomes. Each new data request needs to have a decision purpose,
owner, frequency, and duration of use. Administrative load audits help restore principals and teachers' time
to leadership and learning.
       The second risk is support mismatch. Uniform programs can provide resources that do not match the
root of the problem. Data-driven planning needs to be complemented by local verification and dialogue.
Agencies can group schools according to needs, then provide a support menu. Schools choose with reasons,
but protection indicators and minimum standards are still mandatory. Comparative evaluation is not just
absorption, but utilization and results.
       The third risk relates to safety and sustainability. Time, renovation, relocation, or distance learning
policies need to go through a risk assessment. Schools have hazard maps, evacuation procedures, emergency
communication, contact data, document protection, alternative location plans, and psychosocial support.
Simulations are evaluated based on the time, understanding, and needs of learners, including disabilities.
Coordination with BPBD, health, village, and family must be trained before emergencies.
       The fourth risk is digital exclusion and poor data. Systems can speed up services, but they can also
make it difficult for families without devices, connectivity, or literacy. Help and face-to-face channels
remain available. Learner data is protected, access is restricted, and corrections are easy. Schools should not
punish students for unchecked system errors. Dashboards are used for diagnosis, not permanent labels or
automated decisions without context.
       The fifth risk is aggregate evaluation. District or school scores can mask disadvantaged groups.
Outcome, access, safety, and complaint data are sorted without revealing identities. Percentile time,
variation between regions, dropout cases, building conditions, and experiences of vulnerable groups need
to be reviewed. Findings trigger support and correction, not just ranking.
Model of Strengthening Implementation in Cianjur Regency
       The first stage is the mapping of needs and capacity. The district government combines data on
Education Report Cards, SPM, Dapodik, demographics, facility conditions, distribution of teachers,
transportation access, connectivity, complaints, and disaster risk maps. The data is read at a level that is
detailed enough to find variations, but still protects identity. Schools verify information and explain local
factors. The mapping results are not a ranking list, but rather a typology of needs that directs forms of
support. Updates are made periodically because population changes, damage, teacher mutations, and
emergency conditions can change priorities faster than the annual planning cycle.
       The second stage is policy design with a clear theory of change. Each program describes problems,
root causes, target groups, actions, resources, assumptions, risks, outputs, and outcomes. School
implementers are involved to test feasibility, while students and families help assess access and impact.
Policies are examined in terms of rights, inclusivity, safety, administrative burden, and budgetary capability.
The same provision can provide multiple implementation pathways according to the context, with minimum
standards and clear discretionary boundaries so that adaptation does not turn into injustice.
       The third stage is the distribution of differentiated support. Schools receive not only instruction, but
relevant resource packages: mentoring of principals, community of teachers, technical personnel, assistance
with facilities, connectivity, psychosocial support, or cross-sectoral facilitation. Intensity follows gaps and
risks. The agency establishes the person in charge who can be contacted, response times, and escalation
mechanisms. Support is evaluated from the use and changes in the process, not the number of activities.
Schools that have not been successfully analyzed for causes before being given consequences, so that
capacity issues are not mistakenly treated as non-compliance.
       The fourth stage is phased implementation. New policies are tested on schools with different regional
characters and capacities. Trials look at interpretation, time, cost, workload, family access, safety, and
unplanned impacts. Findings are used to improve guidance, training, systems, indicators, and support prior
to expansion. Policy versions are maintained so that schools are aware of applicable provisions. Transition
periods and termination of old procedures must be clear; without it, schools run two systems at once and
administrative burdens increase.

       The fifth stage is monitoring and rapid learning. The dashboard links implementation to access,
processes, security, and outcomes, and displays variations rather than just averages. Supervisors conduct
evidence-based and observation-based dialogues, while complaint channels capture invisible experiences.
Review meetings result in concrete decisions with those in charge and deadlines. Problems that schools can
solve are addressed close to users; resource or cross-sector issues are raised to the agency and district
governments. Follow-up summaries are communicated so that participation builds trust.
       The sixth stage is resilience and sustainability. Each school policy and plan is tested against disruption
scenarios, including earthquakes, landslides, floods, building damage, power or network outages, and
temporary displacement. Schools set service priorities, alternative locations, family communication, data
protection, role sharing, support for disabilities, and learning recovery. Simulations result in evaluation, not
just documentation. District governments map the shared capacity of BPBD, health, social, village, and
partners so that the response can move without waiting for new coordination when an emergency occurs.


DISCUSSION
       The findings reinforce the theory of implementation that policy is a process, not just a decision. The
Edwards III model emphasizes communication, resources, disposition, and bureaucratic structure, while
other implementation approaches underscore the character of the policy, actors, and context (Edwards, 1980;
Hill & Hupe, 2022). In education, frontline implementers have discretion because the situation of students
and schools is diverse. Quality depends on the ability to direct discretion to the goals and rights of learners.
       School management serves as a translation capacity. Schools receive many policies that can compete
with each other in time, data, and attention. Leaders need to build coherence: choosing the priorities that
affect students the most, connecting activities, and reducing fragmentation. Local governments support
coherence through policy calendars, report integration, a single source of data, and program coordination.
Without it, schools experience implementation overload that lowers quality.
       School-based management provides an argument for close autonomy of users, but the evidence
suggests autonomy alone is not enough. Autonomy requires information, competence, participation,
resources, and accountability. Schools with low capacity can be left behind if all responsibilities are
transferred without support. The differentiation model of support is important for Cianjur because of the
uneven geographical conditions and risks. The standards of rights and safety remain the same, while the
strategy and intensity of assistance adapt to needs.
       The 2025 effective hours policy provides an example of the relationship between design and
implementation. The determination of start times and duration is intended to optimize learning and character
building. However, results need to be assessed through attendance, learning readiness, travel safety, family
burden, actual learning time, and school responses. Evaluation is not a rejection of a policy, but a way to
ensure that goals are achieved and unwanted impacts are corrected. Adaptation space must be accompanied
by evidence and authority.
       The context of a disaster expands the definition of quality. Facilities that are beautiful but not safe do
not meet quality. Schools that achieve learning outcomes but do not have a sustainability plan remain
vulnerable. Post-earthquake revitalization shows the need to link physical development with learning
processes and work competencies. Disaster-safe education needs to enter into governance, contextual
curriculum, simulation, maintenance, and partnerships, not become a once-completed activity.
       The Education Report Card provides an evaluation architecture that brings together learning
outcomes, equity, teachers, processes, climate, and governance. The advantage lies in data-driven planning
and the reduction of separate instruments. However, meaningful use requires data literacy, reflection time,
and the capacity to find the root of the problem. Principals should not be left alone to read dashboards.
Supervisors and learning communities can help interpret and design realistic actions.
       The quality of service needs to be measured from the perspective of students and families along with
professional standards. Satisfaction is important, but learners are not ordinary consumers. Educational
decisions must protect the best interests of the child, fairness, and long-term goals. Schools need to explain
policies, provide participation, and handle complaints, but not change academic standards or protections just
because of pressure. Trust grows from competence, openness, integrity, and fair treatment.

        Cross-sectoral coordination is decisive for children who face multiple barriers. Poverty, health,
disability, population administration, violence, and disasters require referrals. The system needs to establish
a single case manager, consent and data protection, time, and feedback. Citizens should not be interagency
couriers. The success of coordination is measured by completion, not by the number of meetings or
memorandums of understanding.
        The research model generates four propositions. The implementation of quality policies improves
support, consistency, and access. Quality school management improves the learning process, services, and
use of resources. School management mediates the influence of policies on quality. Regional context,
disaster risk, capacity, and data integration moderate relationships. The model can be tested with multilevel
data because individuals are in schools and schools are in district systems.
        The implication of the policy is that the Cianjur Regency government needs to start from the service
problem, not the list of programs. Problems are mapped using data and user voice, then interventions are
selected based on the root cause. Each policy has a theory of change, resources, implementation support,
trials, indicators, and follow-up decisions. Schools are given room for responsible adaptation. This cycle
connects design, implementation, management, and quality as a whole.
        The managerial implication for schools is to establish a routine of improvement. Teams review data,
choose priorities, test actions, observe processes, and adjust. Teachers are involved because class changes
cannot be forced through documents. Students and families provide experiential information. Principals
protect focus, develop competencies, and ensure safety. Results are communicated honestly, including
limitations, so that participation becomes shared learning.
        The contribution of this article is a cross-level model that places quality as the result of the interaction
of policy implementation and school management. The model avoids policy determinism and the tendency
to blame schools. The limitation is that there is no direct empirical measurement. Therefore, the findings
need to be used as a diagnosis and hypothesis framework, not an estimate of the magnitude of influence.


CONCLUSION
       The implementation of education policies affects quality through clarity of objectives,
communication, resources, implementation capacity, coordination, adaptation, and monitoring. A well-
designed policy is not enough if the support is not reached or the context is ignored. In Cianjur Regency,
geographical variation, access, disaster risk, facility recovery, and learning time policies demonstrate the
importance of contextual evaluation and proportional support.
       School management translates policies through data-driven planning, leadership, teacher
management, learning, financing, facilities, student services, partnerships, and quality assurance. The
impact can be seen on access, reliability, responsiveness, certainty, inclusivity, security, relevance, results,
satisfaction, and trust. The influence of policy is mediated by management capacity, while the influence of
management is limited by the quality of system support.
       Alignment between levels is a key finding. District governments need to connect data, policies,
resources, assistance, implementation, user experience, and evaluation in one cycle. Schools need
responsible autonomy and differentiated support. This article does not state statistical coefficients because
it does not use primary data, but provides a model that can be tested and used to strengthen the quality of
Cianjur education services.


RECOMMENDATIONS FOR DEVELOPMENT
       The Cianjur Regency Government is advised to compile an implementation map for each priority
policy, audit the administrative burden, and integrate the Education Report Card with access data, facilities,
disaster risk, complaints, and user experience. Support is grouped according to school needs. New policies
are tested in different regions before being expanded. Each policy has a feedback mechanism, outcome
indicators, protection of vulnerable groups, and corrective decisions. Schools develop a focused plan,
maintain learning time, and train service continuity.

to the variation in the needs of each region in Cianjur Regency.
