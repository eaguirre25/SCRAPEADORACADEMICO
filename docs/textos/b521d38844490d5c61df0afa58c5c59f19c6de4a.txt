Abstract
Education has always been in a state of flux because of technological disruption.
As schools head towards a present in which digital technology is normalised as part
of the fabric of everyday society, a post-digital paradigm, Artificial Intelligence
(AI) is changing the educational administration and leadership. It is crucial to find
ways to coexist with AI while striving for a human-centric future. While numerous
studies advocate for a collaborative synergy between AI and human leaders in edu-
cational governance, there is a gap in explicating the precise roles they undertake.
This paper aims to address the void by providing a conceptual framework for the
symbiotic roles of AI and educational leaders in the administrative decision-making
process. This paper views administrative decision-making in schools as a political
process involving negotiations among administrators, teachers, students, and par-
ents. Within the framework, AI shoulders informational roles such as collecting and
analysing data with its data analysis capability. Conversely, educational leaders are
tasked with establishing the vision, disseminating information, managing conflicts,
identifying new opportunities, negotiating and allocating decision-making author-
ity to stakeholders. Moreover, educational leaders should encourage and supervise
the implementation of AI in schools. The framework serves as a theoretical lens for
prospective studies of AI impacts on education leadership and administration and
bears practical implications for the preparation of future school leaders, contributing
to the creation of a scenario where AI augments, rather than replaces educational
leadership.

Keywords Educational administration · Leadership · AI-human collaboration ·
Technological change · AI-assisted decision-making

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is an overarching ‘umbrella’ term including techniques
such as expert systems, machine learning, neural networks, deep learning, and more
(Baker et al., 2019; Raso et al., 2018; Wang, 2021b; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019).
Among the techniques, machine learning is the most commonly used but is new to
educational administration and leadership (Wang, 2021a). This technique enables
machines to learn from data and replicate or enhance human intelligence through
algorithms and models. In machine learning-powered algorithmic decision-mak-
ing, a specific part of the reality is represented by the statistical model behind the
algorithm, and the outcome is achieved with numeric values and previously defined
steps in the system without human involvement (Krafft et al., 2022). For example,
the algorithms can analyse the student performance data and predict the learning
outcomes without human involvement. In this paper, the term ‘AI’ specifically refers
to machine learning, and ‘AI-assisted decision-making’ refers to the general concept
of engaging machine learning-powered systems in the decision-making process.
   Decision-making is essential to leadership (Johnson Jr & Kruse, 2012). Depend-
ing on the complexity, decisions can be classified into structured, semi-structured,
and unstructured decisions (Simon, 1965). Structured decisions, characterised by
routine and operational nature, can often be autonomously executed by machine
learning with its capacity to process large volumes of data, identify patterns and
generate data-driven decisions. Examples of such decisions include scheduling and
attendance tracking. In contrast, semi-structured and unstructured decisions are typi-
cally more intricate and involve the navigation of trade-offs of diverse stakehold-
ers, such as the adoption of new technologies and parental complaints. Due to the
involvement of uncontrollable factors and multiple stakeholders, semi-structured
and unstructured decisions cannot be automated by AI. Instead, educational lead-
ers must find ways to collaborate synergistically with AI in these decision-making
processes.
   To formulate the symbiotically collaborative framework of AI and educational
leaders through decision-making, we amalgamate Herbert Simon’s trichotomy of
administrative decision-making theory and Henry Mintzberg’s managerial role the-
ory with a consideration of the uniqueness of educational administration and lead-
ership. This collaboration framework serves as a theoretical lens for prospective
investigations into the implications of AI-powered administrative decision-making
on educational leaders. Furthermore, it offers guidance for future research about the
synthesis of AI-human in educational administration and preparing future educa-
tional leaders in an AI-driven era.


The story of technology and educational leadership

Education is a political sociocultural practice that has evolved with the progress
of technological revolutions. Technologies, being the application of scientific
knowledge for practical purposes, have the potential to redefine what constitutes

knowledge and how it should be delivered (Zhao, 2019). During the first industrial
revolution (Industry 1.0), the emergence of steam engines facilitated a transition
from farming and feudal societies to new manufacturing processes. Trains were used
for transportation, and textile and steel industries flourished (Xu et al., 2018). Edu-
cation activities were predominantly focused on agrarian and craft-based economics,
benefiting the elite class. Education aimed to equip individuals with the necessary
knowledge and skills to respond to economic expansion (Seldon et al., 2020; Xu
et al., 2018; Zhao, 2019). During this era, the limited flow of information meant
that the educational administration process was controlled by local authorities,
such as local churches or community leaders, with an emphasis on hierarchy and
standardisation.
    The second industrial revolution (Industry 2.0) emerged with the invention of
electricity, the internal combustion engine, and advancements in steel produc-
tion, which led to the use of oil and electricity to drive mass production (Xu et al.,
2018). These changes brought about the ideas of efficiency and self-achievement.
The enhanced flow of information through telegraphs during this era led to a greater
standardisation of educational leadership. Alongside the emergence of neoliberal-
ism, which advocates free-market principles and limited intervention. This era wit-
nessed mass and institutionalised education with standardised curricula and exams.
The objective of education shifted towards the production of a skilled and special-
ised workforce (Birkinshaw, 2020; Harvey, 2007; Hill & Kumar, 2009; Kumar &
Hill, 2012; Lynch et al., 2012; Seldon et al., 2020). Consequently, education admin-
istration and leadership underwent professionalisation to accommodate a larger edu-
cation system.
    The third industrial revolution (Industry 3.0) is characterised by digitalisation and
automation. Computers, the internet, and other digital technologies revolutionised
human activities. Robots were also increasingly integrated into human manufactur-
ing processes, particularly assisting with repetitive tasks. Digital learning platforms
like Moodle, along with other multimedia resources were introduced into schools.
Digitalisation led to the proliferation of communication technologies and informa-
tion exchange, enabling educational leaders to rely on data and make data-driven
decisions. Meanwhile, the neoliberal principles developed predominantly glob-
ally. Privatisation of education for profit became prominent, leading to disparities
both within and across nations (Hill & Kumar, 2009; Kumar & Hill, 2012). Busi-
ness management theories are incorporated into education administration and lead-
ership to manage educational systems in a business-oriented way, emphasising the
rational and efficient behaviours of individuals (Hanson, 1976; Lundberg, 1962).
The changes necessitated a service-oriented and student-centred model in education
(Schwab, 2017; Seldon et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2018). However, during Industry 3.0,
digitalisation was seen as separate from human activities, with digital technologies
viewed simply as tools to improve efficiency and expand the education industry.
    We currently inhabit the fourth industrial revolution (Industry 4.0), charac-
terised by the integration of AI and other cutting-edge technologies, including
robotics, cloud computing, the Internet of Things (IoT), and big data. The fusion
of these technologies blurred the line between the ‘physical, digital and biological
spheres’ so that people move between the digital domains and offline reality with the

connected technology in daily life (Saucedo-Martínez et al., 2018; Schwab, 2017;
Xu et al., 2018). This revolution witnesses AI collaborating with or independently
performing intelligent tasks alongside humans, such as personal tutoring and auto-
motive assessment in education. The unprecedented volume of data also demands
that educational leaders develop data literacy and new skills to effectively manage
large-scale and real-time data.
    With an increasing integration of AI into human activities, the initiative of the
fifth industrial revolution (Industry 5.0) was proposed. Industry 5.0 complements
Industry 4.0 by integrating social and environmental concerns into technological
innovation, seeking to harmonise human and machine strengths to create a more
‘human-centric, sustainable, and resilient’ future (Xu et al., 2021, p. 532). Concur-

sation is no longer about technological innovation or improvement but is taken for
granted and requires more discussions around its broader social, cultural and philo-
sophical impacts (Jandrić et al., 2018; Peters et al., 2023).
    Under the post-digital condition, the boundary between AI and humans in deci-
sion-making becomes blurred (Macgilchrist, 2021). For example, leaders often
refer to online databases to gather information and insights before making deci-
sions. Although the use of AI systems in educational administration is limited
(Baker et al., 2019), AI-powered tools are integrated into leaders’ daily roles, often
without conscious awareness. AI’s ability to self-learn and the reliance on big data
transforms it from a neutral technology into a technology with social and political
implications, thereby triggering issues regarding privacy, transparency, inequality,
and the alterations of power dynamics in human activities (Williamson, 2023). This,
in turn, makes AI an active agent that ushers a new era of education in which the
intricate relationship between machine learning and educational leaders is redefined
(Gulson et al., 2022). Therefore, the inclusion of AI in the decision-making process
with educational leaders necessitates developing an AI-leader collaborative model
to explore their evolving roles and responsibilities, which may result in the emer-
gence of novel roles, to address this changing landscape (Baker et al., 2019; Gulson
et al., 2022).


AI in human leadership: augmentation in decision‑making

The engagement of machine learning in decision-making at the administrative level
is not unique to educational institutions; it is also employed to aid leaders in vari-
ous other organisations. Studies by Jarrahi (2018), Wang (2021a), and Shrestha
et al. (2019), have investigated the symbiotic modes of AI and humans in different
decision-making contexts. Jarrahi (2018) discerned that AI systems exhibit height-
ened efficacy in addressing intricate issues within analytical decision-making sce-
narios, whereas humans should spearhead intuitive decision-making processes when
dealing with uncertain and equivocal predicaments. Wang (2021a) asserts that AI
systems excel in data-driven or evidence-based decisions, while human judgment
is better suited for value-based decisions. Shrestha et al. (2019) proposes that AI

systems can function autonomously, devoid of human intervention, in situations
necessitating precise, rapid, and repetitive outcomes.
   These AI-human collaboration models explore how AI can augment human per-
formance with its vast computing power in data recognition while ensuring humans
maintain decision-making authority. Machine learning’s ability to handle large data-
sets and identify intricate patterns allows AI systems to surpass humans in data pro-
cessing (Wang, 2021a). This proficiency in cognitive analysis also reshapes the cog-
nitive processes and behaviours in administrative activities (Gulson et al., 2022). To
understand how leaders collaborate and coexist with AI systems in decision-mak-
ing, we must examine the specific aspects and ways in which machine learning can
enhance human leaders’ capabilities. Clarifying the specific roles and responsibili-
ties of both AI and human leaders through the decision-making process can reveal
how they can complement each other.
   Despite the various studies exploring modes of AI-human collaboration in deci-
sion-making, there remains a dearth of research elucidating the distinct roles that
AI, and human leaders assume in the process, especially with a consideration of
the school ecosystem and educational leadership. This paper aims to fill this gap by
providing a synergetic collaborative framework between AI and educational lead-
ers to demonstrate how they can coexist in the decision-making process of educa-
tional administration within the school system by clarifying their respective roles
and responsibilities.


Theoretical background

The school ecosystem is distinct from other non-educational organisations, and con-
sequently, educational leadership and decision-making possess unique characteris-
tics. In schools, the most fundamental tasks and goals revolve around teaching and
learning activities, and student achievement (Millikan, 2018). Student achievement
is influenced by complex factors that reciprocally intricate with each other, referred
to as the ‘Ecological Model’ (Bronfenbrenner, 1994). In the innermost layer of the
school system, which includes the immediate surroundings, the student is directly
influenced by teachers, parents, and school administrators. Beyond this, extracur-
ricular activities and religious involvement connect the school to the broader soci-
ety, playing a significant role. Expanding further, the broader socioeconomic envi-
ronment, including government policies and community services, also impacts the
student’s development. Finally, societal and cultural norms shape the broader edu-
cational context and exert their influence as well (Ryan, 2001). When educational
leaders aim to improve teaching and learning practices and student achievement
through decision-making, they must navigate the nuanced decisions influenced by
diverse interest groups within these subsystems, such as students, teachers, commu-
nity members, and so on.
    Therefore, instead of having a rigid hierarchical structure in decision-making,
schools exhibit a decentralised structure, in which leaders, teachers, students, par-
ents, government, and community resources are interconnected with and influence
each other through the decision-making process (Wallin, 2014). For example, when

developing a new curriculum, a committee involving leaders, teachers, students, par-
ents, and community members is formed. Committee members gather information
from their groups and discuss findings and proposals with other members, and the
decision is made through consensus. In the process, the educational leader’s role
extends beyond merely making the final choice but must integrate various require-
ments and preferences from the other stakeholders and respond to competing ten-
sions to make effective decisions (Evers & Lakomski, 2013).
    Understanding the characterisations of the educational ecosystem and leadership,
this paper will deconstruct the decision-making process based on Herbert Simon’s
trichotomy and clarify the roles of leaders employing Henry Mintzberg’s managerial
roles. The two theories will be integrated to shed light on the roles and responsi-
bilities undertaken by leaders through the complicated decision-making trajectory,
considering the unique features of educational institutions. Simon is renowned for
deconstructing the administrative decision-making process, and it provides a general
model that applies to most contexts, including schools. The concept of ’bounded
rationality’ in his theory reflects the nature of human decision-making in reality.
AI systems, to some extent, can extend the boundaries of human capabilities and
thereby complement the decision-making process. On the other hand, understanding
and articulating leaders’ roles is challenging due to their complex and multifaceted
nature. Mintzberg’s role theory addresses the most basic question—‘What do lead-
ers exactly do?’ by programming the unstructured roles of leaders. When Mintz-
berg originally developed his model, he emphasised the thoughts of the decisional
school, which has its origins in Simon’s work and is particularly relevant due to its
intricate nature (Mintzberg, 1973). Although these two theories were not originally
designed for educational studies, their integration, considering the uniqueness of
the school ecosystem and educational leadership, can provide an applicable frame-
work for the educational context. This theoretical synthesis aims to establish the
essential groundwork for the AI-human collaborative role framework in educational
leadership.


Herbert Simon’s administrative decision‑making theory

In economics, ‘decision-making’ encompasses making choices with associated
consequences under uncertainty (Edwards, 1954; Schrenk, 1969). In Simon’s deci-
sion-making theory, the economic perspective of decision-making assumes an ideal
scenario by the ‘economic man, who possesses complete rationality, exhaustively
pertinent information, and a consistent preference for maximising the outcome’
(Edwards, 1954, p. 381). However, real-world organisations typically involve indi-
viduals tasked with decision-making responsibilities, referred to as ’administra-
tive men’ (Simon, 2013). These individuals operate under what Simon termed
the ‘satisficing principle’. This principle involves prioritising alternatives that are
deemed ’good enough’ rather than exhaustively optimal due to constraints imposed
by limitations in information, cognitive capacity, time, and personal values (Melé,
2010; Mintrom, 2015; Simon, 2013). This cognitive and informational limitation
is regarded as ‘bounded rationality’ (Simon, 2013). Consequently, the practice of

decision-making by administrative individuals evolves as a social process, charac-
terised by group dynamics and interpersonal considerations, extending beyond mere
information about alternative consequences. This phenomenon is called ‘administra-
tive decision-making’ (Vroom & Yetton, 1973).
   Administrative decision-making represents an intricate interplay of two insepa-
rable constituents—factual and value-based elements (Melé, 2010; Simon, 1965).
The factual dimension pertains to the rational and technical facets of decisions,
encompassing scientific and practical insights that inform the decision and its con-
textual environment (Simon, 2013). However, the factual model of decision-making,
influenced by the ‘administrative man’, tends to overlook the impact of social and
cultural factors (Lundberg, 1962). This necessitates the inclusion of value-driven
elements, such as ethical judgment, social values, intuitions and personal biases.
Decision-making entails the preferences for future alternatives of groups or individ-
uals. Therefore, the value-based elements are significant, and the presumptions of
decision-makers (Simon, 1965). The dichotomous nature makes administrative deci-
sion-making a political activity, responding to both internal and external environ-
ments (Cray et al., 2007; Schrenk, 1969). Within the organisation, the decision-mak-
ing process adheres to the hierarchical structure where values of the higher positions
usually are delivered to the lower positions (Simon, 1943). Employees with diverse
expertise collaborate through decision-making to pursue organisational objectives
(Simon, 2013). Outside the organisation, decision-making entails negotiations and
trade-offs among diverse interest groups (Cray et al., 2007; Harrison & Pelletier,
2000; Shrestha et al., 2019). Hence, the factual and value-based elements are not
mutually exclusive but rather intrinsic dimensions of administrative decision-mak-
ing (Melé, 2010).
   Drawing on Simon’s model of administrative decision-making, the trajectory can
be divided into three stages—the intelligence stage, the design stage, and the selec-
tion stage. During the intelligence stage, objectives and problems are identified. The
design stage represents the informational juncture. Herein, feasible solutions are
crafted based on the available information, often involving extensive information
search and the generation of alternatives. In the subsequent selection stage, vari-
ous alternatives are subjectively evaluated by stakeholders, leading to the ultimate
choice. This choice is reached through a combination of evaluation and negotiation,
reflecting the interactions and discussions among stakeholders (Mintzberg et al.,
1976; Simon et al., 1987).
   Simon’s trichotomy can be effectively applied to the educational decision-making
process. The decision-making structure in schools is often decentralised. A decen-
tralised model of decision-making enables the involvement of administrators, edu-
cators, parents, students, and local community members (Mutchler & Duttweiler,
1990). Collectively, these stakeholders engage in the analysis, generation, and evalu-
ation of prospective solutions concerning various dimensions of the school system,
such as staffing, budget allocation, curriculum design, instructional strategies, and
beyond (Elacqua et al., 2021; Mutereko & Chitakunye, 2015). In addition, Lakomski
and Evers (2022) call for attention to the role of emotions in educational decision-
making, emphasising that emotions are deeply intertwined with rational decisions.
The labels we assign to emotions are socially constructed, and thus can motivate us

to make value statements to different decisions and thus choose the optimal choice.
Emotions significantly influence educational decisions as leaders and teachers invest
emotionally in students’ development. Therefore, in educational decision-making,
the leader is expected to integrate the viewpoints of stakeholders and harmonise
the dichotomy between facts and values through the participation and negotiations
among stakeholders, while adhering to Simon’s tripartite framework.


Henry Mintzberg’s managerial role theory

The responsibilities shouldered by leaders are inherently complex and multifaceted.
To gain insight into the roles of leaders in decision-making, Mintzberg’s managerial
role theory is employed. In the model, leaders’ unstructured roles are programmed,
outlining a classification of 10 roles that managers undertake (Mintzberg, 1973).
It is important to note ‘managers’ and ‘leaders’ are different– managers primarily
navigate daily operational intricacies, while leaders catalyse and rally their teams
towards the achievement of organisational objectives. This paper narrows its focus
to the behaviours and roles displayed by leaders. The terms ‘leaders’ and ‘managers’
are used interchangeably.
    The roles are categorised into three domains: interpersonal, informational, and
decisional roles. The interpersonal roles encompass the figurehead, the leader, and
the liaison. Due to the interchangeability of the terms ‘leader’ and ‘manager’ in this
paper, the figurehead and liaison roles will be discussed. As the figurehead, leaders
embody a charismatic presence, contributing to the establishment of organisational
culture and vision. They forge connections with both internal subordinates and exter-
nal stakeholders. In the role of a liaison, leaders orchestrate an equilibrium between
individual requisites and organisational objectives. The second type of role—infor-
mational roles—enables the leaders to serve as the ‘nerve centre for information’ to
receive and deliver information, facilitating the exchange between the internal and
external environment. In the informational role, as a monitor, leaders sift through
and accumulate information. Subsequently, they disseminate this information to
internal teams as a disseminator while also articulating value statements. Further-
more, leaders play the role of spokesmen, preaching factual and value-laden infor-
mation to external interest groups. With the information collection and dissemina-
tion, the leaders bear the third type of roles—the decisional roles—the entrepreneur,
the disturbance handler, the resource allocator, and the negotiator. As entrepreneurs,
leaders catalyse organisational changes and innovations. They are also disturbance
handlers, addressing unforeseen incidents and reconciling stakeholder conflicts. In
their roles as negotiators, leaders also strike harmony among stakeholders. Addi-
tionally, leaders act as resource allocators, allocating decision-making authority to
participants and employing organisational resources.
    The uniqueness of school ecosystems determines the distinctiveness of educa-
tional leaders, who wield a unique mediating impact on the ultimate organisational
outcome: student achievement. This influence is often deemed ‘second only’ to the
effects of teaching and learning itself (Leithwood & Riehl, 2007). Effective edu-
cational leaders ought to adopt a forward-thinking view of education and play a

vital role in establishing goals, visions, and cultures in the schools (Gurr & Drys-
dale, 2020). Simultaneously, educational leaders focus on providing tailored sup-

Integration of the two theories in education

When educational leaders are making semi-structured and unstructured decisions,
their roles go beyond merely making a final selection; they encompass a wide range
of activities, including interpersonal interactions, information transfer, and negotia-
tions (Mintzberg, 1973). The educational decisions are influenced by various factors
and stakeholders, such as parents, teachers, co-curriculum activities, social and cul-
tural norms, government policy and so on. The leaders need to establish the vision
and overall goals for the school, manage relationships with the stakeholders, allocate
decision-making power, and balance stakeholders’ values. They also need to allo-
cate resources throughout the process and encourage other stakeholders to engage in
decision-making (Elacqua et al., 2021). With the complex nature of semi-structured
and unstructured decision-making in schools, the decision process and leaders’ roles
do not conclude at the final phase. Instead, they often cycle back to the initial step,
where new problems emerge, and the process needs to be repeated.
   To gain a comprehensive understanding of an educational leader’s roles through-
out the administrative decision-making process, Simon’s trichotomy theory and
Mintzberg’s managerial theory are integrated, with a consideration of the unique-
ness of the school ecosystem (See Fig. 1). This integration allows us to clarify the
distinct roles that a leader assumes at each step of the decision-making process.
   In the intelligence stage, the educational leader serves as a figurehead to set up
the educational objectives. Additionally, the leader acts as the resource allocator to
encourage the teaching staff and students to collaborate in identifying the problems
that need to be addressed. Furthermore, the leader functions as a liaison and a dis-
seminator, ensuring that other stakeholders are well-informed about the issues to be
decided upon.
   During the design stage, the educational leader plays a crucial role in collecting
and disseminating information, as well as making value-laden statements to guide
the decision-making process. In this phase, the educational leader encourages the
active participation of other stakeholders, fostering collaborative brainstorming to
generate innovative alternatives. Therefore, the leader encompasses all the informa-
tional roles, such as a monitor, a disseminator, and a spokesman, in addition to tak-
ing on certain decisional roles, such as the role of an entrepreneur and a resource
allocator.

roles in domains related to human nature, such as creativity, empathy, curiosity, and

liaisons, and disseminators, facilitating the communications and connections with
teaching staff, students, and parents are paramount. Although AI can effectively
collect and analyse the data, it is human leaders who undertake the roles of moni-
tors, disseminators, and spokesmen, effectively communicating the information to
stakeholders. Leaders must hold an entrepreneurial stance when addressing novel
challenges as the education culture is mobilising, especially in an AI-driven era
(Andrew, 2017). This magnifies the role of the entrepreneur during the design. After
the alternatives are generated with the aid of AI, educational leaders are tasked with
gathering stakeholders and harmonising their interests, fostering networks and col-
laborations (Kolbjørnsrud et al., 2016; Sterrett & Richardson, 2019; Tyson & Sau-
ers, 2021). This aligns with the roles of spokesmen, disseminators, disturbance han-
dlers, and negotiators (Breuer & Szillat, 2019; Kolbjørnsrud et al., 2016; Tyson &
Sauers, 2021). The role of educational leaders as resource allocators is significant
since they must allocate decision-making authority to stakeholders and empower
teachers, students and parents who understand the nuances of classroom dynam-
ics to respond to changes in a broader school community (Brynjolfsson & McAfee,
2014; Dewhurst & Willmott, 2014; Sharma et al., 2023). Therefore, the role of the
resource allocator is in each step of the decision-making trajectory (Kolbjørnsrud
et al., 2016).
    The advent of AI systems introduces novel leadership roles as well, including
those of motivators and monitors (Johansson & Björkman, 2018). Leaders must gain
an in-depth understanding of AI system performance, recognise new opportunities
it presents, assess when and how to use the technology, and wisely choose the right
tools to align with the school framework (Agrawal et al., 2017; Davenport, 2018;
Tyson & Sauers, 2021). They also need to motivate and inspire the teaching staff to
adapt to and be comfortable with using AI systems and oversee their integration into
daily routines. This includes designing, monitoring, and supporting the AI used in
ways that fit organisational goals and contribute to improved decision-making pro-
cesses (Richardson et al., 2021; Sterrett & Richardson, 2019).
    When the boundary between AI and educational leaders is blurred, it is essential
to find a way for the leaders to coexist with AI by integrating AI’s capabilities into
the decision-making process. The framework provides a conceptual roadmap illus-
trating the distinctive roles assumed by AI and human leaders within the context of
semi-structured and unstructured administrative decision-making and clarifies how
AI and human leaders can contribute to and collaborate within the educational deci-
sion-making trajectory. This is the first and paramount step to establishing a coexist-
ing future between AI and educational leaders.

Conclusions

The conceptual framework indicates the distinct roles assumed by educational
leaders and AI systems within the trajectory of semi-structured and unstructured
administrative decision-making. This is achieved by synthesising Simon’s trichot-
omy of administrative decision-making theory and Mintzberg’s theory of mana-
gerial roles while also considering the unique nuances of the school landscape
and educational leadership. Educational administrative decision-making must
integrate the requirements and preferences of diverse stakeholders, as it is a polit-
ical process involving teachers, students, and parents. The remarkable capacity
for data analysis and computation equips AI systems to undertake informational
roles, such as the collection and analysis of factual data during the design phase.
Conversely, human leaders are tasked with human-centric roles and articulate
value-laden statements when necessary. These roles include interpersonal dimen-
sions to establish the educational vision for the schools, informational dimensions
including disseminating information to stakeholders, as well as decisional dimen-
sions encompassing bringing in new opportunities for the schools, dealing with
conflicts, negotiating, and allocating decision-making authority to stakeholders.
Furthermore, novel responsibilities for human leaders include supervising the
ethical conduct of AI systems and fostering the adoption of the technology among
teachers and students. The framework clarifies the roles and responsibilities AI
and educational leaders undertake through the decision-making process, aiming
to enhance the synergetic collaborations between them. It also provides a basis
for prospective inquiries into the impacts of machine learning on educational
administration and leadership, offering practical implications for the preparation
of future school leaders. The framework contributes to the scenario in which AI
augments, rather than replaces, educational leaders and thus advocates for a more
human-centric future.
